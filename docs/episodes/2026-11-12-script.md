## New Jersey Politics

That's a notable dip, and it comes even as New Jersey Monitor reports that younger, more diverse voters were actually a major force behind this fall's broader Democratic wave statewide, while older voters who'd backed Trump in past cycles stayed home. Put those two data points together and the picture is mixed. Young voters who did turn out helped drive the result, but fewer of their peers are signing up to vote at all.

## Tech and AI

The biggest story in tech right now isn't a product or a merger. It's a possible beheading of the world's most valuable chipmaker, and it's moving fast.

Nvidia's board is reportedly preparing to oust founder and CEO Jensen Huang, according to Semafor and the Wall Street Journal, with directors meeting as early as Wednesday to decide his fate. The trigger was a second reported extramarital affair. The New York Times reports Huang was involved with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of health care, who reports to him directly. That's on top of an earlier reported affair with a woman U.S. intelligence has suspected of spying for China. The FBI is now investigating, according to Ars Technica, and Nvidia's board has briefed the White House on its plans, according to a senior administration official cited by Semafor.

The board doesn't have an obvious successor lined up. Bloomberg reports Nvidia has hired an outside search firm to find Huang's replacement, and one name under consideration is Johny Srouji, Apple's chief hardware officer. Some directors are reportedly favoring Nvidia's chief financial officer, Colette Kress, as an interim chief while that search plays out. John Gruber of Daring Fireball is skeptical the Srouji idea goes anywhere, noting Srouji publicly denied wanting to leave Apple just last year, and was promoted to his current role only months ago. Gruber also points out there's more at stake here than Huang's personal conduct. He cites reporting that roughly 20 percent of Nvidia's chip sales may have been illegally smuggled into China, something the company is accused of covering up. For a company whose market value dwarfs most national economies, this is a governance crisis unfolding in real time, with no clear line of succession and federal investigators now involved.

Away from the Nvidia drama, Taiwan's TSMC says it will invest one billion dollars in both Anthropic and OpenAI. Ars Technica frames this as another example of the circular financing propping up the AI boom, where chipmakers, cloud providers, and AI labs keep funneling money into each other even as confidence in the broader AI trade wobbles.

Google DeepMind has unveiled AlphaGenome 2, a new AI model built to show scientists how changes in DNA affect individual cells. The goal is to give researchers a clearer view of the biological roots of disease, with hopes it could point toward new treatments down the line.

Separately, Google DeepMind says it used AI to help mathematically verify key parts of the Signal messaging protocol. The project took four weeks and suggests AI tools could make formal software verification, normally a slow and expensive process, far more practical for other critical systems.

United Airlines says it won't let AI agents book flights on behalf of customers, citing privacy and regulatory concerns. The airline says it hasn't yet seen many travelers actually attempt this, echoing what other companies like DoorDash have reported.

And in Washington, a federal court has ruled against the Trump administration in its bid to access journalists' private LinkedIn messages. The court said confidential sources are, in its words, the lifeblood of a free press, a win for press protections as the government pushes back against surveillance of reporters' communications.
