## New Jersey Politics

A new audit out of Trenton is raising real questions about your electric bill. State regulators reviewed Atlantic City Electric Company's books and found evidence the utility overcharged customers and violated state tax law while rates were climbing. The audit covers a five year period, and it opens the door to possible refunds for ratepayers. Regulators are expected to dig further into how widespread the compliance failures were.

Former Governor Tom Kean marked the twenty fifth anniversary of the September 11th attacks with a warning. Kean, who chaired the federal 9/11 Commission after leaving the governor's office, said some of the vulnerabilities identified after the attacks still concern him today. He's using the anniversary to push officials not to grow complacent on security.

New Jersey's schools are facing a tough report card. A new review finds nearly 40 percent of students who earn high school diplomas in the state can't handle basic high school level math. Education researchers are calling it a false sense of readiness for college, since a diploma isn't translating into the skills colleges expect.

In Trenton, a labor fight is expanding into youth and amateur sports. Business groups and sports officials are pushing state lawmakers for a law that would formally classify referees and umpires as independent contractors rather than employees. It's the latest front in a broader worker classification debate playing out in the state legislature.

Also in Trenton, parents of children with rare pediatric conditions known as PANS and PANDAS are lobbying state lawmakers to mandate insurance coverage for treatment. These are neuropsychiatric syndromes that can appear suddenly in children, and families say insurers are currently refusing to cover care. They're asking the legislature to require coverage statewide.

New Jersey has also joined a multistate lawsuit against the Trump administration over a new federal rule on green cards. The rule could block green card approvals for immigrants who might use public benefits like Medicaid. Attorney General Matt Platkin's office argues the rule oversteps federal authority and will hurt mixed status families. Separately, a related federal public charge rule is expected to push some mixed status immigrant households to forgo enrolling their U.S. citizen children in public benefits altogether, out of fear it could jeopardize a family member's immigration case.

At the federal level, the U.S. Supreme Court stepped into the fight over mail voting. The justices kept in place a lower court order blocking a Trump administration plan to impose new restrictions on mail ballots ahead of this year's midterms, siding with Democratic led states that challenged the rule.

And Senate Democrats say they've uncovered a separate Trump administration operation that combed through state voter rolls looking for noncitizens allegedly voting in federal elections. The senators allege the effort may have broken state laws in the process. It's a federal inquiry, not a state one, and it's likely to draw more scrutiny from Capitol Hill in the coming weeks.

## Markets

The story dominating the bond market right now is a selloff that's now spilled into stocks, gold, and even how Wall Street is pricing the future of artificial intelligence spending. The ten-year Treasury yield climbed to its highest level since 2007, a milestone in what's now a nearly two-decade high. That move came during Monday's US session, when a rally in oil prices deepened the pressure on bonds and dragged stocks lower, with investors bracing for a Federal Reserve decision expected as soon as this week.

Oil is a big part of why. Francisco Blanch, the commodities and derivatives research head at BofA Securities, said Brent crude could climb as high as 150 dollars a barrel if critical infrastructure, like Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, gets hit on a more permanent basis. Higher oil feeds directly into inflation expectations, which is one reason gold slipped below four thousand three hundred dollars an ounce, as traders bet the higher energy costs will reinforce the case for the Fed to keep rates elevated rather than cut.

That yield surge is also raising alarms about how AI is being financed. Kokou Agbo-Bloua, the global head of economics, cross-asset and quant research at Societe Generale, warned that if the ten-year yield keeps climbing toward five and a half to six percent without a matching pickup in growth, it could break the economics behind debt-fueled AI capital spending. Separately, a Bank of America survey found that fear of a disorderly bond move, combined with uncertainty around the US midterm elections, is already trimming investor bullishness. The consultancy Marsh is responding by moving some client portfolios back toward a neutral Treasury allocation, arguing elevated yields are now attractive enough to buy into.

In deal news, Spain's Puig Brands has agreed to buy out the remaining 50 percent of skincare company Isdin that it didn't already own, in a deal valued at one point two billion euros, or about one point four billion dollars. The purchase gives Puig full control of the brand. Puig shares fell on the news, which is showing up in European trading as that market sits mid-session.

Finally, in the UK, employers cut payrolls by 26,000 workers in August, according to the Office for National Statistics. That's the fastest drop in nine months and far worse than the 5,000 decline economists had forecast, though these early estimates often get revised. The weak reading feeds into a broader split in Europe, where traders are increasingly betting that the European Central Bank and the Bank of England won't be able to raise rates as far as policymakers themselves are signaling, given how much labor markets are already cooling.

## Tech and AI

Sam Altman, Dario Amodei, and Demis Hassabis all put their names on something unusual this week: a call to slow down.

Amodei, the Anthropic CEO, published a lengthy essay called "We Must Pace the Frontier," arguing AI development is moving too fast for safety to keep up. Over the weekend, Altman of OpenAI, Hassabis of Google DeepMind, and Elon Musk of SpaceX loosely signed onto the same idea, agreeing to "pace the frontier" rather than race ahead unchecked. Skeptics jumped on it almost immediately, suggesting the pledge looks less like a safety pact and more like the industry's biggest players agreeing among themselves to slow the field just as competition from smaller labs heats up. Lina Khan, the former Federal Trade Commission chair, weighed in too, arguing regulators already have the legal tools to hold AI companies and their executives accountable, pointing back to enforcement precedent from 1934. And from Beijing, China's intelligence chief added his own warning, casting AI as a serious risk and calling for the country to tighten its grip on what officials are calling technological sovereignty.

Nvidia's Jensen Huang offered a different kind of AI messaging on Monday. Speaking at the All-In Podcast's summit, Huang put President Trump on speakerphone in front of the crowd. During the call, Trump told the audience that robots aren't going to take over the world, a lighter counterpoint to the anxious tone coming out of the safety debate the same week.

Microsoft spent part of the week cleaning up after itself. This month's Patch Tuesday update fixed nearly a thousand security flaws, Microsoft's largest patch batch ever, but it also broke things. Businesses reported problems with Hyper-V folder shares, and users flagged issues with audio, remote access, and even copy and paste. Microsoft has now pushed an emergency out-of-band update to undo the damage from its own record-breaking fix.

Apple, meanwhile, shipped its own major software wave. iOS 27 and the newly renamed macOS Golden Gate 27 are now out, bringing a refreshed Siri built on Apple Intelligence and refinements to the Liquid Glass interface Apple introduced last year. It's also the last version of macOS that will run older Intel apps through Rosetta. Apple Home is getting AI upgrades too, with video summaries for security cameras, but that feature comes at a cost, with some tiers running as high as sixty dollars a month.

Volvo used the week to update two of its bigger plug-in hybrids. The 2028 XC60 and XC90 are getting larger batteries, pushing their electric-only range well beyond what most plug-in hybrids offer today, and both are getting new infotainment systems built around Google's Gemini. The smaller XC40 is also getting a design refresh for 2028, with new lighting and body details.

In gaming hardware, Valve's new Steam Frame headset is out, but at a higher price than the company originally hoped. Valve says global shortages in RAM and storage pushed the cost up from its original target. As for a next-generation Steam Deck handheld, Valve says it's still working out how and when that might happen, with no timeline yet.

Two space companies are fighting over Mars. Rocket Lab, the launch company, is formally challenging NASA's decision to award a seven-hundred-million-dollar Mars communications contract to Jeff Bezos's Blue Origin, arguing the award doesn't match NASA's own eligibility criteria. Separately, the Pentagon confirmed for the first time that the United States has weapons already deployed in orbit, describing them as space control systems meant to defend military forces operating there.

On the security front, a maximum-severity bug in GitLab is now under active attack, according to the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, with researchers spotting scans against exposed servers just days after a patch went out. And the banking app Revolut says it was fooled by fake government requests into handing over customer passports, selfies, and transaction histories, with attackers now demanding ten thousand bitcoin.

Finally, Oracle capped off what it's calling a banner quarter, driven largely by AI cloud demand, with another round of layoffs. And the Trump administration's Environmental Protection Agency moved to scrap the remaining federal limits on greenhouse gas emissions from power plants, a decision that comes as AI data centers are already driving electricity demand higher across the country.
