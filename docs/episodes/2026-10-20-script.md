The package is still moving through committee, so expect more debate before anything reaches the governor's desk.

## Markets

French government bonds are flashing a warning sign this morning, and investors are watching closely before Wall Street even opens. A closely tracked measure of French bond risk breached a key level during Tuesday's European session, which is still underway as of this broadcast. The move underscores how hard it's becoming for the French government to tame its budget deficit while facing cost-of-living protests at home. Wei Li, from the BlackRock Investment Institute, said the selloff is a reminder that a broader fight for capital is putting government borrowers under heavier scrutiny, especially with yields elevated worldwide. That dynamic means investors are demanding more compensation to hold French debt, pushing borrowing costs higher just as Paris needs to convince markets its fiscal plans are credible.

Meanwhile, the rally in artificial intelligence stocks lost momentum during Monday's US trading session, and that weakness is still weighing on sentiment ahead of Tuesday's open. Equities wavered and bonds gained as the AI rebound sputtered. The trigger was a rough debut for a South Korean data center company, whose stock drop reignited doubts about whether the massive spending boom behind AI infrastructure can actually keep paying off. That single listing was enough to revive broader worries about whether valuations across the AI trade have run ahead of the technology's real earnings potential, and futures trading is reflecting that caution heading into the US session.

Also, in Britain, unemployment fell to four point seven percent in the three months through August, a better than expected reading. The drop gives Chancellor of the Exchequer John Healey an unexpected boost just a week before he delivers his budget. Stronger jobs numbers give him a bit more room to argue the economy is holding up, even as he faces pressure to address Britain's finances in that upcoming budget statement.

## Tech and AI

Apple has sent out invitations for a second Los Angeles event this month, set for October 28th. Rumors point to a new MacBook Pro with an OLED touchscreen, an entry level M6 MacBook Pro, an M6 iMac, and an iPad mini with its own OLED display. The invite itself played off Apple's new tagline, calling the event "Pro and Tell." That follows Time magazine's new cover story on CEO John Ternus, who took over after Tim Cook and oversaw last month's launch of the foldable iPhone Duo. Ternus told Time that Apple is deliberately staying out of the heaviest AI spending race among its peers, betting instead on a smaller, more private approach to AI built into its own devices.

That spending race is exactly what's rattling investors elsewhere. CoreWeave, the cloud computing company that rents out AI computing power, saw its bonds fall into distressed territory last week. That happens when investors demand at least ten percentage points more yield than they'd get on government bonds, a sign they think the company might struggle to pay its debt. The investor Chamath Palihapitiya flagged the slide and asked publicly whether the broader AI buildout amounts to a private credit bubble. The concern is that so much of the AI infrastructure boom is being financed with debt rather than cash, and if demand for AI computing doesn't keep up, that debt becomes a problem for lenders across the industry, not just CoreWeave.

Powering all that AI computing is also straining the electric grid. A new report finds the United States is building more fossil fuel power infrastructure than it has in over a decade, even as renewable energy growth slows down. Electricity demand is climbing at its fastest pace in more than ten years, driven by data centers, factories, and electric vehicles. Amazon is trying to address its own piece of that demand by funding a new nuclear plant in Indiana, built with the firm X-energy and American Electric Power. The plant would replace a retired coal station and aims to supply up to 960 megawatts of carbon-free power, though X-energy has not yet completed a working reactor. Meanwhile, in the UK, Microsoft has walked away from a planned AI supercomputer project in Essex, after delays connecting it to the power grid threatened to push the timeline into the next decade.

On the policy side, the federal government has moved to block Chinese AI service DeepSeek from all civilian agency systems. The Register reports the order banning its use is vague about exactly what counts as a violation, leaving agencies to figure out the details themselves.

Disney has reportedly found a way to acquire Character.AI, the chatbot company, without taking on its legal liabilities. Character.AI is facing lawsuits alleging its bots encouraged minors to self-harm or harm their parents. How Disney structured the deal to avoid inheriting those claims wasn't fully detailed, but the separation itself is the notable part of the story.

Also in AI security, federal investigators say a former OpenAI engineer accused of spying for Saudi Arabia had a folder on his devices labeled with Saudi government documents. The FBI is reportedly concerned the Saudi government could help him leave the country before facing the spying charges.

In the business world, President Trump announced that Elon Musk's planned giveaway of SpaceX stock to American children will be distributed through so-called Trump Accounts. Musk had said he would give one SpaceX share to every kid in the country sometime next year, and thanked Trump-account backer Brad Gerstner for helping arrange it.

Netflix has lost its film chief, Dan Lin, following last week's announcement that the company is cutting six percent of its global staff, roughly a thousand jobs. The cuts concentrated in product, technology, legal, and human resources teams. Lin's exit comes as incoming chief content officer Peter Rice takes over from Bela Bajaria, who is also leaving the company. Netflix has acknowledged slowing growth and falling viewer engagement.

In space news, Japan has launched its Martian Moons Exploration mission, aiming to bring back a physical sample from Phobos, one of Mars's two moons.

## Soccer and the World Cup

Folarin Balogun, the American forward, is at the center of a governance fight brewing between Europe and FIFA over how the World Cup gets run.

UEFA, European soccer's governing body, has sent FIFA a formal letter demanding answers to 42 questions. FIFA has two weeks to respond. The questions cover Gianni Infantino's plan to partially sell off the World Cup to private investors, FIFA's Peace Prize, and the overturned red card shown to Balogun during this summer's 2026 tournament. UEFA wants to know how that reversal was decided and why.

Meanwhile, Infantino himself is focused on holding onto his job. His campaign for reelection as FIFA president moves to Japan this week, with a meeting scheduled for Wednesday with the Asian Football Confederation. He's working to lock down support across Asia even as UEFA presses him for answers back in Europe.

## the NFL

The biggest story in Philadelphia right now has nothing to do with a play on the field. Jalen Hurts, the Eagles quarterback, is expected to miss the rest of the season. Doctors found multiple blood clots in his lungs, according to NFL Network and ESPN. The team and Hurts's agent, Nicole Lynn, issued a joint statement Monday confirming he's done for the year. Looking back, it's striking Hurts was even able to play in the team's Week 5 game given what was developing in his body. Medical experts who spoke with ESPN say blood clots in the lungs, known as pulmonary embolisms, can be serious and typically require blood thinners and extended recovery time, which is why a return this season looks unlikely.

The timing could hardly be worse for Philadelphia. The Eagles have now lost four straight games for the first time under head coach Nick Sirianni, falling to 2-4 after Sunday's defeat to the Panthers. Beat writers covering the team didn't hold back afterward, with one Bleeding Green Nation analysis calling for backup quarterback Sean Mannion to be replaced and describing the offense in blunt terms. There's also been public discussion about whether trust has frayed between Hurts and the organization over how his health was handled leading up to the diagnosis.

All of that uncertainty now bumps up against the league's scheduling office. The NFL had until Tuesday to decide whether to flex the Eagles' Week 8 matchup against the Commanders out of prime time. With Philadelphia at 2-4 and Washington sitting at 1-4-1, that game had been trending toward a flex given both teams' struggles this season.

Washington had its own rough night Monday, falling 23-17 to the previously unbeaten 49ers, who improved to 6-0. San Francisco led by as much as 20-3 at halftime behind touchdowns from George Kittle and Christian McCaffrey, before the Commanders clawed back behind two touchdown passes from quarterback Jayden Daniels. Washington had two separate drives deep into 49ers territory in the final two minutes but couldn't finish the comeback.

The Commanders took a bigger hit than just the scoreboard. Wide receiver Terry McLaurin was carted off with his left leg in an air cast after a first-quarter knee injury and was ruled out for the rest of the game. He's expected to undergo further testing. San Francisco also lost some firepower when wide receiver Mike Evans left with Achilles soreness he'd been managing all week, after missing practice Thursday, Friday and Saturday. Coach Kyle Shanahan said afterward he's hopeful Evans can play Sunday against the Falcons and doesn't believe the setback is long-term.

Elsewhere around the league, the Rams are dealing with a significant loss of their own. Wide receiver Davante Adams ruptured his hamstring in Sunday's defeat to the Cardinals and will be out six to ten weeks, according to head coach Sean McVay. Los Angeles will also be without defensive tackles Poona Ford, who goes on season-ending injured reserve, and Kobie Turner for an extended stretch. McVay said running back Kyren Williams was simply emotional after the loss as the team tries to explain its offensive inconsistency, with the Rams now sitting last in the NFC West.

In Indianapolis, owner Carlie Irsay-Gordon fired general manager Chris Ballard on Monday, a move that's raising questions about whether a broader front-office overhaul is coming as the trade deadline approaches. Radio host Dan Patrick reported the Colts could even explore bringing in franchise legend Peyton Manning as general manager.

And on the business side, Amazon's Thursday Night Football continues drawing strong audiences. The Seahawks-Broncos game on Prime Video pulled in sixteen point two six million viewers, according to Nielsen's official numbers, extending a trend of growing audiences for the streaming package.
