## Local News

A federal lawsuit filed by New Jersey touches home directly this week. Attorney General Jennifer Davenport joined twenty-one other state attorneys general and Pennsylvania's governor to sue the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development over a new rule that would strip federal housing aid from families with mixed immigration status. Under the rule, a household that includes even one person without legal status, even if other members are U.S. citizens, would lose assistance entirely and could face eviction. The lawsuit, filed in federal court in Massachusetts, argues the rule reverses nearly three decades of federal policy and violates the Administrative Procedure Act. Davenport said the rule would put children on the street. The rule is set to take effect December 14th unless blocked.

Representative Analilia Mejia, who represents New Jersey's 11th district, praised Governor Sherrill's administration and Attorney General Davenport for a separate move, a motion to enforce New Jersey's lawsuit against the Department of Homeland Security over its plan to convert a warehouse in Roxbury into an ICE detention facility. Mejia said that when she questioned a DHS secretary under oath on November 18th, he claimed the agency had no timeline for the site, even though DHS had already awarded a contract to design it for detention use. Mejia said the governor's office had been pushing DHS for weeks to turn over that contract, which happened only recently. The state is now asking a federal court to compel DHS to halt work at the site.

New Jersey's Office of New Americans says nearly five thousand residents received free legal help over the past fiscal year, including detention and deportation defense and assistance for unaccompanied minors. About twenty-four hundred low-income residents got help fighting deportation, and another twenty-four hundred young people were served through the program for unaccompanied minors, which filed more than two hundred state court petitions on behalf of abused or abandoned children. ## Tech and AI

A federal jury in New York has reached a verdict in one of the biggest corporate criminal cases in years. Jurors found Huawei, the Chinese tech giant, guilty of stealing trade secrets, laundering money, and violating US sanctions by building internet infrastructure for Iran. The case grew out of a 2020 Justice Department indictment and included evidence that Huawei engineers copied notes from Cisco's source code, even referring to Cisco internally as "ocsic," its name spelled backward. The jury did not find Huawei guilty of running an illegal racket. The verdict closes out a trial that ran for three months in the Eastern District of New York and also touched on the stolen technology of T-Mobile and Motorola.

Nvidia's Jensen Huang is pushing back on the story of his own reinstatement. During his first all-hands meeting since returning as chief executive, Huang reportedly told staff it's "completely untrue" that President Trump was directly responsible for getting him his job back, despite Trump publicly claiming credit. Huang said politics played no role and called Nvidia "the most apolitical company." That claim is complicated by new reporting from The Wall Street Journal describing a frantic 48-hour White House campaign, led by administration officials, to pressure Nvidia's board into reversing course after Huang's departure over scandal tanked tech stocks. Separately, financial disclosures show Trump himself may have made as much as 15 million dollars on his own Nvidia shares after the stock surged following Huang's return, a jump that added roughly 950 billion dollars to the company's value in a single day. Washington followed up on the Huang saga in another way, too. The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security added Megaspeed, the Singaporean cloud provider at the center of the scandal that triggered Huang's brief ouster, to its trade blacklist, along with several affiliated firms in Singapore, Malaysia, and Thailand, citing concerns about advanced chips being diverted to China.

Microsoft is overhauling how it responds to major outages after internal memos described what Reuters characterized as a kind of engineering fog of war. The company's Microsoft 365 president, Jason Henderson, called the November 12th outage that knocked out Exchange Online, Teams, and Outlook the most damaging incident in Exchange Online's history. Microsoft's own post-incident report traced the failure to an undetected scalability defect introduced back in July, which caused lock contention in gateway processes. A Teams traffic failover then piled extra load onto servers that were already strained, partly because maintenance had taken almost 14 percent of gateway capacity offline. One fix alone reportedly took five to six days to roll out worldwide, far short of Microsoft's one-hour target for urgent security patches.

CrowdStrike is making the largest acquisition in cybersecurity history. The company has agreed to buy Zscaler, a network security firm, for roughly 43.9 billion dollars in an all-stock deal, with Zscaler shareholders getting 1.135 CrowdStrike shares for every share they hold. That's a 34 percent premium over Friday's closing price, and it surpasses Google's 32 billion dollar purchase of Wiz as the sector's biggest deal. CrowdStrike says combining its endpoint protection with Zscaler's zero-trust network tools will let it track what AI agents are doing across corporate networks. Customers are already bracing for higher renewal costs.

The Treasury Department's financial crimes unit has its own security problem to deal with. FinCEN disclosed that it's investigating a cybersecurity incident involving unauthorized access to a limited subset of its systems, and has taken some systems offline as a precaution. The disclosure came hours after the New York Times reported a separate breach affecting an open-source intelligence system used by the CIA and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence.

Amazon has new evidence that AI is changing offensive hacking, not just defense. The company said it caught AI agents finding and exploiting a zero-day vulnerability with almost no human guidance, beyond a single instruction to gain root access on a server at an East Asian university. The agents were running on an open-source penetration-testing framework, given a list of IP addresses and told simply to get shell access. They found a previously unknown flaw in a medical imaging application and used it to take full control of the system. Amazon said the prompts were written in simplified Chinese with the system clock set to China Standard Time.

VMware has issued its third critical security bundle in a single week, this one addressing 23 separate issues, including a flaw rated 9.8 out of 10 that lets attackers bypass authentication entirely and take over vCenter, the tool administrators use to manage virtual machines. VMware acknowledged the timing, landing right before the Thanksgiving holiday in the United States, isn't ideal, but said the pace of disclosures reflects how fast AI-assisted threats are moving.

There's turnover at two major enterprise tech companies. Nutanix CEO Rajiv Ramaswami announced he's stepping down once the board finds a successor, saying he has no other job lined up yet. And Dell and HPE both told investors this week that they consider themselves fundamentally different companies than they used to be. HPE's shift follows its acquisition of Juniper Networks, while Dell says its infrastructure unit, now up 198 percent year over year to 42 billion dollars in revenue, has become the source of most of its profit and growth.

In Africa's tech and telecom sector, several deals are reshaping the competitive map. In South Africa, Naspers-owned Takealot grew revenue 27 percent to 571 million dollars over six months as it fends off Amazon, Shein, and Temu, with earnings up 21 percent in local currency terms. MTN, Africa's largest mobile operator, is reportedly back at the table with Telkom on a takeover four years after an earlier attempt collapsed, a deal that could combine two mobile networks and a major fibre operator but also raises competition concerns for smaller rivals. And in Nigeria, cybersecurity startup Aeon extended its pre-seed funding round to 2 million dollars, with Launch Africa Ventures and Octerra Capital joining existing backers, to expand its engineering team and build out its cloud security tools.

## Women's News

The Post reported that Tulane awarded Tate Weston a degree in 2022 while he was under investigation for raping fellow student Cameron Lewis. University administrators had assured Lewis that Weston would not be allowed to graduate while the disciplinary case was open. He got his diploma anyway, months before Tulane found him responsible for the assault. A New Orleans grand jury indicted Weston this year on a charge of second-degree rape. He pleaded guilty earlier this month to felony second-degree battery and misdemeanor sexual battery under a plea deal, receiving three years of probation with no requirement to register as a sex offender. Students gathered at Tulane last Friday demanding the university revoke his degree, and Cameron Lewis, now a graduate student there, spoke to the crowd about her experience with the university's disciplinary process.

Keisha Lance Bottoms, the former mayor of Atlanta, made history this month as the first Black woman elected governor of any U.S. state, winning in Georgia. Bottoms has said she wants to overturn Georgia's six-week abortion ban. But her win is drawing mixed reactions even from voters who back that position. One Georgia voter, writing for Rewire News Group, said she backed Bottoms specifically because of abortion rights, while arguing that Bottoms's record as Atlanta mayor complicates the symbolism of her victory.

A pair of reports from the Council on Criminal Justice looked at what happens when women spend less time in prison. The reports found that cutting women's prison time in half in Illinois and North Carolina could save tens of millions of dollars a year combined, with only modest increases in arrests. The researchers also pointed to costs that rarely get counted, including children separated from mothers, grandparents taking on caregiving, and families losing income when a woman is incarcerated.

## the NWSL

Offseason moves are piling up across the NWSL, starting in Washington. The Spirit fired head coach Adrián González on Monday, just two days after losing the NWSL Championship to the Kansas City Current. That loss made it three straight championship defeats for González, who went 39 and 13 with 18 draws across all competitions. He first reached a final as an assistant under Jonatan Giráldez in 2024, then lost two more as head coach in 2025 and 2026. All three of those championship losses were decided by a single goal. The Spirit also fell to Club América in the Concacaf W Cup final back in May, a defeat that cost them a spot in the 2028 Club World Cup. Spirit president of soccer operations Haley Carter said the club believes a change in leadership is needed to reach its long-term goals. Owner Michele Kang has built one of the league's most talented rosters, and reporting from The Athletic indicates the absence of actual silverware factored directly into González's dismissal.

Gotham FC is also reshaping its roster. Goalkeeper Ann-Katrin Berger, last season's NWSL Goalkeeper of the Year, is leaving the club to join Union Berlin in Germany's Frauen-Bundesliga. Berger spent three seasons with Gotham, winning an NWSL championship in 2025, the Shield in 2026, and the Concacaf W Champions Cup in 2025. She made 87 appearances for the club and posted 33 shutouts, including 11 clean sheets this past season alone, making her the first NWSL goalkeeper to log ten or more shutouts in back-to-back seasons. Her departure follows defender Jess Carter's move to Bay FC earlier this month, meaning the reigning Shield winners have now lost two key starters this offseason.

On the international stage, FIFA has named the United States, Mexico, Jamaica, and Costa Rica as co-hosts of the 2031 Women's World Cup. FIFA plans for the U.S. to host the majority of matches, marking the first time the tournament returns to American soil since 2003. U.S. Soccer president Cindy Parlow Cone said the goal is a tournament that inspires players around the world. Host cities haven't been announced yet, though markets including Atlanta, Denver, Seattle, Kansas City, Orlando, and Houston have submitted bids.

And in a look back at the championship itself, Dan Lauletta of The Equalizer flagged a pair of historical notes. Vanessa DiBernardo scored the first goal directly from a corner kick in NWSL Championship history, something only done once before in final history, by Tobin Heath back in 2013. And Debinha became the first player to score in three different NWSL Championships, with her third career final goal tying her with Amy Rodriguez, Crystal Dunn, and Jess McDonald for the most ever.
