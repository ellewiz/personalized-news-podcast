## New Jersey Politics

New Jersey's lowest-paid workers are getting a raise, courtesy of inflation rather than a new law. Starting January first, the state's minimum wage climbs by more than fifty cents an hour, landing at sixteen dollars and forty-eight cents. That increase comes automatically under New Jersey's existing wage law, which ties yearly adjustments to the rate of inflation. Some smaller employers and seasonal workers fall under separate exceptions, but for most hourly workers in the state, this is now locked in for next year.

There's also a shake-up atop New Jersey's election system. Dale Caldwell resigned Friday as lieutenant governor and as secretary of state, the post that oversees how elections are run statewide. His departure followed an internal ethics investigation. Governor Mikie Sherrill has since been reshuffling election leadership in response, and she flagged how important that oversight role is at a moment when voting administration is under scrutiny nationally.

That scrutiny is playing out at the county level too. The American Civil Liberties Union of New Jersey is now in a dispute with Ocean County's Republican election leadership, accusing officials there of trying to illegally purge Democratic and unaffiliated voters from the rolls. The ACLU argues the challenge process being used violates state or federal voter protections. It's a fight worth watching, since Ocean County has been a flashpoint in past ballot access disputes.

On housing, Governor Sherrill is looking to expand a state program that puts unused government-owned land toward new development. New Jersey Transit has previously sold off land for housing construction. This time, the state wants to hold onto ownership of the land itself while still allowing units to get built on it, a shift in approach aimed at keeping public control over property even as it's developed.

Finally, a Statehouse-adjacent race worth noting, even though it's a federal contest rather than a state one. In New Jersey's Seventh Congressional District, incumbent Republican Tom Kean Junior once built his campaign around attacking his opponent's stock trades. Now his own trading activity is under the microscope, as his new Democratic challenger, Rebecca Bennett, is making it a central line of attack heading into next year's race.

## Markets

The banking world in Italy is bracing for a vote that could reshape the country's financial landscape. Luigi Lovaglio, the chief executive of Banca Monte dei Paschi di Siena, told Bloomberg Television he's confident shareholders will back his twin takeover offers for Banco BPM and Banca Generali. Lovaglio described the plan as building what he called an Italian champion. He needs support from at least two thirds of votes cast at a shareholder meeting scheduled for October 29th to move forward. The stakes are high for Italy's banking sector, which has seen limited consolidation compared to other European markets.

Meanwhile, a major private equity financing deal has closed with an unusual twist. A group of banks led by Bank of America and Deutsche Bank agreed to a rare lender protection to push through a financing package for Platinum Equity's acquisition of a stake in Nestle's water business. The deal is valued at two point eight billion euros, or roughly three point two billion dollars. The concession from the banks suggests lenders saw enough risk in the transaction to need extra assurances before signing off.

In Europe, where markets are open and trading through the morning, France delivered an inflation reading that's pressuring the European Central Bank. French consumer prices rose three point four percent in September compared to a year earlier, up from two point six percent in August. That's the fastest pace in more than two years, driven largely by surging oil and natural gas costs. Statisticians at Insee, France's national statistics agency, pointed to energy as the main culprit. The reading adds to pressure on the ECB to keep raising rates, even as a separate measure of French government bond risk hit a new milestone on investor worries about political instability heading into next year. Traders are pricing in the possibility of a populist government loosening fiscal policy, and that combination of hot inflation and political risk is keeping French debt markets on edge. Back in the US, where trading hasn't opened yet, futures are holding steady ahead of the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge and earnings from Micron Technology, two data points investors are watching closely for signals on interest rates and the artificial intelligence trade.

## Tech and AI

OpenAI had a busy stretch, and not all of it went the company's way. At its DevDay keynote, OpenAI unveiled Dots, an always-on AI assistant meant to compete with Meta's Muse, capable of working across connected apps in the background while it learns a user's habits. But the same week, OpenAI pulled its planned GPT-6.1 Astra model from release, citing security trade-offs the company says make it too risky to ship. Reports also indicate the model had trouble knowing when to stop once it started a task, a side effect of training it to keep working rather than quit early. Outside the venue, protesters organized by more than a dozen advocacy groups rallied under banners reading "People Over Profit," and elsewhere activists installed a sculpture depicting AI executives fleeing a sinking ship. Meanwhile, OpenAI's chief executive, Sam Altman, said the company still has no plans to go public, telling reporters an IPO won't happen until OpenAI can make firmer promises about the safety of its models.

Anthropic, the AI company behind the Claude chatbot, took a different approach in its own leaked IPO paperwork. According to documents reported this week, Anthropic is warning prospective investors that its models could resist being shut down and potentially cause catastrophic harm, framing existential risk as part of the pitch rather than something to downplay.

That unease echoes through a new set of interviews from Palisade Research, a nonprofit that studies artificial intelligence. Researchers, including current and former employees of OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Anthropic, appeared in videos arguing that advanced AI is exactly as dangerous as it sounds. One of them, Geoffrey Irving, a former OpenAI and DeepMind researcher, put the odds of human extinction from AI at roughly a coin flip.

The Trump administration, for its part, is steering federal messaging in the opposite direction. A new executive order directs government agencies to stop using the term "artificial intelligence" altogether, replacing it in official documents and websites with "Super Intelligence." The administration also secured signatures from several major tech companies on a set of AI safety commitments that critics describe as vague and non-binding. And a new government portal, America dot gov, is now using chatbots built on Google's Gemini and xAI's Grok as the main interface for federal resources, though early reviews call the site unfinished and difficult to navigate.

On the corporate side, AMD announced it's acquiring World Labs, an artificial intelligence startup, in a deal worth eight point two billion dollars, expected to close by the end of the year. It's widely seen as an attempt to challenge Nvidia's dominance in AI computing hardware. Separately, investors appear to be pricing in real productivity gains from AI. Economists analyzing stock movements estimate markets are anticipating a thirty two point six percent productivity boost for software engineers from AI tools, though they caution that markets can overshoot on hype.

In cybersecurity news, Dutch police announced the arrest of a twenty four year old man in Amsterdam suspected of leading ShinyHunters, a hacking group tied to breaches at Ticketmaster, Rockstar Games, and more recently the FBI itself. The arrest reportedly took place two weeks before the group claimed its FBI breach, raising questions about whether the suspect was even still involved by that point. The FBI has since put out its own pointed message to the group, essentially telling remaining members that investigators know how to track them down.

Turning to space, SpaceX's Starship finally reached orbit, deploying twenty six Starlink V3 satellites, though engine trouble cut the mission short. The vehicle came home after roughly three hours instead of the planned ten. Boeing, meanwhile, is getting another chance at ferrying astronauts, with its Starliner capsule now expected to fly again as soon as January, and a crewed mission targeted for 2028 if testing goes well. And Airbus completed the first flight of its new A350 freighter, a plane the company says is well suited to hauling heavy cargo like server equipment.

On the health front, a clinical trial found that retatrutide, an experimental obesity drug that mimics three different hormones instead of just one, helped participants lose up to twenty five percent of their body weight, making it the most powerful weight-loss drug tested so far.

And in smaller items, BMW detailed its redesigned i3 electric sedan, now rated for up to four hundred sixty eight miles of range. It turned out to be a false match.

## Soccer and the World Cup

A teenager wearing Borussia Dortmund's colors just rewrote a page of American soccer history. Mathis Albert, a 17-year-old forward, scored for the United States men's national team in a 4-2 win over Chile in St. Louis on Tuesday. That goal made him the youngest scorer in USMNT history, breaking a record previously held by Christian Pulisic.

Albert wasn't the only teenager making noise. Julian Hall also scored in the win, giving the U.S. two teenage goal scorers in the same match. The night also belonged to a more familiar name working his way back into form. Sebastian Berhalter, the USMNT midfielder, recorded a goal and an assist, his second straight match doing both. Defender Chris Richards anchored a young, shaky backline and helped steady the team after they fell behind early.

The win came with a farewell attached. Tim Ream, the veteran Charlotte FC defender, announced his retirement from the national team. Ream had long been a steadying veteran presence in the U.S. setup, and his exit leaves that experienced leadership role open as this young core continues to develop.

Briefly, in club football, Manchester City were found guilty by the Premier League on more than one hundred charges tied to financial rule breaches spanning the 2009 to 2018 seasons. Investigators say the club artificially inflated commercial revenue by roughly one point one billion dollars. City says it plans to appeal before Friday's deadline.

## the WNBA

Caitlin Clark just made WNBA history in the middle of an elimination fight. She dropped at least 25 points and 15 assists in Tuesday's Game 2 against the Aces, a stat line no player has ever produced in a single game, playoff or regular season. The Fever needed every bit of it, holding off Las Vegas 99-89 at Gainbridge Fieldhouse to even their first-round series at one game apiece and force a decisive Game 3.

That win didn't come without drama. Aces center A'ja Wilson had to be pulled away by her own teammates late in the game after confronting a fan she said had stepped toward her and pointed at her. Wilson picked up a technical foul in the process and afterward called the fan's behavior unacceptable.

Across the bracket, the Liberty closed the book on the top-seeded Lynx. New York, the number eight seed, completed a sweep of Minnesota in their best-of-three opener, becoming the first eighth seed in league history to knock out a number one seed in the opening round. Lynx head coach Cheryl Reeve didn't sugarcoat it afterward, saying her team overachieved this season but simply doesn't have the right group for a deep playoff run right now. It's the third straight year Minnesota's season has ended in playoff heartbreak.

The postseason exit came just after Reeve picked up a very different honor. She won her fifth WNBA Coach of the Year award, a record no other coach in the league's history has matched, after guiding the Lynx to 33 wins during the regular season.

Two more major awards went out this week as well. Angel Reese, the Chicago forward now playing her first season with the Dream, was named the Associated Press Defensive Player of the Year. And Lynx guard Olivia Miles was named Rookie of the Year, winning the honor by a unanimous vote after a debut season the league is already calling record-breaking.

Elsewhere, the league's newest franchise took a moment to look back. Players on the Toronto Tempo, the WNBA's expansion team, shared the moments that defined their inaugural season, with head coach Carlie Nurse saying she was proud of how thoroughly Canada embraced the team in its first year.

And in Chicago, the Sky closed out their own season review. Head coach Tyler Marsh pointed to the team's young core as the clearest bright spot from a season with plenty of both good and bad to sort through.
