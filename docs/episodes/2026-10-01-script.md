## New Jersey Politics

New Jersey's public media landscape just shifted. NJ Spotlight News, the policy and politics outlet that's covered Trenton for sixteen years, has moved into a new home at Montclair State University, joining forces with NJ PBS. The outlet says its brand, its mission, and its reporting team all stay intact under the WNET ownership umbrella. For a listener who relies on deep state policy coverage, that's worth knowing, since it's one of the main outlets tracking Trenton on a daily basis.

In Trenton itself, a new bill is moving through the state legislature that would expand Daniel's Law. That's the statute letting police officers and judges in New Jersey shield their home addresses and other personal information from public records. Right now it covers in-state officials. The proposed expansion would make out-of-state judges and law enforcement officers eligible for the same protections. The law was originally passed after the murder of a federal judge's son at her New Jersey home, and lawmakers have broadened it more than once since.

Also out of Trenton, an op-ed in NJ Spotlight News is pushing the state to rethink how it handles inflation. The argument is that New Jersey's tax brackets don't adjust when prices rise, unlike roughly a dozen other states that index their brackets to inflation. The piece argues that a one-time rebate check doesn't fix the underlying problem the way automatic bracket adjustments would.

Meanwhile, the legal fallout over abuse in New Jersey's youth detention system keeps growing. Thirty more people have sued the state, accusing officials of running what their complaint calls a culture of abuse inside New Jersey's youth jails. These filings add to a wave of nearly five hundred lawsuits now pending against the state over conditions in those facilities.

Switching to Washington, Senate Democrats blocked a Republican-led bill meant to restrict stock trading by members of Congress. The procedural vote failed fifty-three to forty-seven, breaking entirely along party lines. Democrats said the bill's restrictions were too weak to actually stop lawmakers from trading on inside knowledge, so they voted it down rather than let a watered-down version move forward.

And still at the federal level, Democrats say President Trump is using twenty million dollars in Homeland Security funding to pay for television ads that prominently feature him, ahead of this year's midterm elections. Democrats raised the funding source as a concern, arguing taxpayer money meant for homeland security is being redirected into what amounts to political advertising.

## Markets

Bond markets are sending a loud signal this morning, and oil is a big part of why. Crude has pushed back above one hundred dollars a barrel, and that's rippling through everything from Treasuries to European equities.

The US ten-year Treasury yield has climbed to its highest level since 2002, as a selloff in government bonds spreads globally. Rising oil prices are feeding inflation fears, and investors are demanding more return for holding long-term debt. In London, the effect is even starker. The UK's thirty-year borrowing cost hit six percent for the first time since the euro crisis gripped markets back in 2012, making Britain the first major economy to pay that much on long-term debt in nearly three decades. That pressure is already showing up on the London Stock Exchange, where the FTSE 100 is trading lower this morning, dragging broader European stocks down with it as the session continues.

Not everyone sees this as bad news for bond investors. Jim Bianco, the macro strategist who runs Bianco Research, told Bloomberg Television he's bullish on Treasuries even as yields rise. He argues the higher yields reflect stronger nominal growth and firmer inflation, and he's putting money into bonds for the first time in six years. Bianco said the ten-year could climb toward five and a half to five and three-quarters percent, but added that yields near five and a quarter percent already look attractive to him.

Meanwhile, hedge funds are positioning for a weaker euro, loading up on options tied to political and fiscal risks building in France. That currency bet is another sign that traders expect this bond turmoil to keep rippling across markets, not just in Britain and the US.

Away from the bond rout, London is bracing for what could be its biggest initial public offering in five years. Airtel Money, the mobile payments firm, is looking to raise five hundred twenty-nine million pounds, worth about seven hundred million dollars, from shareholders. A listing of that size would be a notable vote of confidence in London's IPO market, which has struggled to attract big-name offerings in recent years.

Ahead of the US market open, futures are mixed. Wednesday's session saw US stocks struggle for direction, as gains in artificial-intelligence-linked shares fought against the pull of those surging bond yields. On the labor side, there was a bright spot. Outplacement firm Challenger, Gray and Christmas reported that US companies announced the fewest job cuts for any September since 2022, a sign the labor market is holding up even as borrowing costs climb.

## Tech and AI

Google dropped a new frontier AI model today, and almost nobody can touch it. The company unveiled Gemini 4 Argon, which its DeepMind engineering lead, Koray Kavukcuoglu, says delivers frontier performance on complex software engineering, legal and finance work, and cybersecurity defense. But Google is restricting access to what it calls trusted cyber defenders for now, meaning the general public and most developers can't actually use it yet.

That caution sits oddly next to the money pouring into the industry. A new estimate making the rounds says the AI sector needs to generate six trillion dollars a year in economic value by 2031 just to justify the infrastructure spending already committed. Anthropic's IPO filing gives a sense of the scale involved. The company reported a net loss of forty-two billion dollars in 2025, and says it plans to spend five hundred eighteen billion dollars on cloud and computing commitments in the years ahead. Revenue did grow twelvefold last year to nearly four point six billion dollars, but nearly a quarter of that came from just two customers, neither locked into long-term contracts. Meanwhile OpenAI has delayed its own IPO over safety concerns and is instead seeking another thirty billion dollars in private funding.

The Trump administration's answer to AI risk is to let the industry grade its own homework. Dozens of AI companies have agreed to voluntary safety testing under a new White House plan, with no independent enforcement mechanism attached.

On the hardware side, Micron's chief executive says RAM supply is about to get worse, not better, even as the company posts sharply higher revenue, profit, and margins. He's framing the price surge as something to celebrate, which tells you who's winning in this market and who's paying for it. In China, Huawei's chairman, Eric Xu, claims the company's homegrown AI chips are now outselling Nvidia domestically. Apple is also expected to refresh the HomePod mini for the first time since 2020 and introduce a new set-top box.

## the WNBA

The Valkyries had shut down the Wings' transition game in Game 1, built around the league's top transition defense against the league's top transition offense.
