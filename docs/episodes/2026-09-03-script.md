## New Jersey Politics

Back-to-school season in New Jersey now comes with a new rule attached. A state law taking effect this year requires every public school district, including those serving Ewing Township students, to ban cell phone use from the first bell to the last. School boards still get to decide exactly how they enforce it, whether that means phones locked away in pouches or just kept in backpacks all day. But the mandate itself is not optional. It's a statewide policy set by Trenton, and it applies to every district in Mercer County and beyond.

New Jersey is also pushing a fight over sports betting all the way to the nation's highest court. The state is asking the U.S. Supreme Court to review whether states have the authority to regulate sports-style contracts sold on prediction market platforms like Kalshi. These platforms let people trade on the outcome of sporting events in a way that looks a lot like a bet, but they've argued they're regulated as financial products at the federal level, not as gambling under state law. New Jersey disagrees, and wants the justices to settle who actually has jurisdiction over these markets.

Finally, researchers in New Jersey are testing a new strategy against rising seas along the coast. At the Scotch Bonnet wetland, a marsh that had started disappearing, scientists piled on sand and soil to rebuild its elevation. Early signs suggest the marsh's natural balance is returning. It's a small-scale experiment, but one that could shape how the state approaches wetland loss elsewhere as sea levels keep climbing.

## Markets

The story dominating trading desks right now is the bond market, and the numbers are striking. Global government bond yields have climbed to their highest levels since 2008. The 30-year US Treasury touched 5 percent this week, a level that's been rare for well over a decade. That spike came right before Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced a surprise expansion of his department's bond buyback program, and right after Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh delivered a hawkish speech at the Jackson Hole symposium. Andrew Sheets, global head of fixed income research at Morgan Stanley, says investors remain skeptical that Bessent's buyback moves can meaningfully change that bigger picture. Still, some money managers see opportunity in the selloff. British asset manager Schroders says it's been buying more 10-year Treasuries, betting yields are close to peaking. And overnight, the broader retreat in bonds slowed as those decade-high yields proved tempting even with energy markets still on edge.

Currency traders, meanwhile, are watching the yen. It jumped more than 1 percent against the dollar, extending a rally into a second straight session and reversing a month of gradual decline. Behind the move, traders are raising bets that the Bank of Japan will hike its benchmark rate by a quarter point later this month, according to people familiar with the central bank's thinking. There's also growing unease about possible intervention from Japanese authorities to support the currency further. That yen strength, combined with a softer dollar, helped push gold higher for a second day. Gold also got support from comments by President Trump suggesting any renewed conflict with Iran would likely be short-lived, comments that eased some safe-haven demand even as Tehran claimed fresh retaliation. Oil prices held steady on that same back-and-forth.

## Tech and AI

The Verge notes this is meant to reflect how deeply artificial intelligence has reshaped the company's operations. Analysts have wanted this Azure number for years, and now they'll finally get it.

Google had a mixed day in two very different arenas. On the product side, the company released Gemini 3.8 Flash, its third Flash-branded model in six weeks. Ars Technica reports it scores well and runs cheaply, even as updates to Google's higher-end Pro model appear to have stalled. On the legal side, a US court ruled that Google will not be forced to sell its ad exchange, despite the Justice Department proving the company acted illegally in an antitrust case. It's a partial win for Google. Prosecutors got the legal finding they wanted, but not the breakup remedy.

The security world spent time this week worried about AI models behaving less like tools and more like attackers. Separately, The Register reported on a real ransomware attack in which AI agents carried out every step themselves, then left the victim an eighty-page security audit afterward. And in a survey cited by The Register, nearly two-thirds of US national security professionals said they believe current AI risks, and the government's posture toward them, are unacceptable. Researchers are now looking at extreme fixes, including so-called data diodes, hardware that only lets information flow one way, as a defense against AI systems that might otherwise hack their way out of a sandbox.

That backdrop makes a separate hire look awkward. Anthropic has brought on Matt Clifford, the architect of the UK's AI policy, to lead the company's dealings with governments outside North America.
