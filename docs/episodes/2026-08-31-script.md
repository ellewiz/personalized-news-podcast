## New Jersey Politics

Property tax bills aren't the only place inflation quietly drains New Jersey wallets. State income tax brackets tell the same story. New Jersey is one of the few states that doesn't adjust its tax rate tables for inflation, a practice known as bracket creep. As wages rise to keep pace with the cost of living, more of that income gets pushed into higher tax brackets, even though real purchasing power hasn't actually grown. One state lawmaker in Trenton has pushed for more than a decade to fix this by indexing the brackets to inflation, the way many other states already do. The proposal has repeatedly stalled. The practical effect is that New Jersey residents pay more in state income tax over time without the Legislature ever voting to raise rates directly.

In Newark, questions are swirling around a school contract worth seven hundred thousand dollars. Newark Public Schools awarded the deal, for hotel and food-service training, to a local mortician best known for embalming Whitney Houston and other celebrities and political figures. Critics want to know how someone with no apparent background in hospitality education landed a contract in that field. The story raises broader questions about how the Newark school district vets and awards its vendor contracts.

Finally, on the national stage, voting rights advocates gathered in Washington, D.C. on Friday to mark the anniversary of the 1963 March on Washington. Union leaders, interfaith organizers, and family members of original marchers rallied to argue that the fight for voting access remains unfinished. The event was a national demonstration, with no specific New Jersey angle attached to it.

## Markets

Wall Street is still working through the fallout from one speech delivered at Jackson Hole, and it's touching almost every corner of global markets this morning.

Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh used his address on Friday to make fighting inflation his priority, and traders took that as a signal the Fed could raise interest rates before year end. That hawkish tone is showing up across asset classes in early trading. Gold extended its slide, adding to Friday's drop of more than three percent, as investors dial back bets on rate cuts. US Treasury yields ticked lower even as stock futures traded mixed, with investors still trying to figure out how seriously to take Warsh's inflation warning. Not everyone is convinced a hike is actually coming. Bond investors at firms including ABN AMRO Investment Solutions and Brandywine Global Investment Management say the market may be getting ahead of itself, and Richmond Fed President Tom Barkin, speaking from Jackson Hole, suggested the Fed's communication approach is still evolving rather than settled. Europe's session is underway right now, and strategists there, including Wolf von Rotberg at J Safra Sarasin, say the speech hasn't actually changed their outlook, because they were already positioned for hikes in September and December.

Oil is climbing in early trading after tensions flared in the Middle East. The United States and Iran exchanged strikes for the first time in about a month, and Tehran says a tanker was hit by mines in the Strait of Hormuz, one of the world's busiest oil chokepoints. That's pushing crude prices higher as traders price in the risk of further disruption to shipping through the strait.

Meanwhile, the yen has broken through 160 per dollar, a level that's now put Japanese authorities on intervention watch. That move happened during the Asian trading session, which wrapped up hours ago, but it's still setting the tone for currency markets heading into the US open. Analysts say the breach shows just how vulnerable the yen remains to further weakness, raising the odds that Tokyo steps back into the market to slow the slide, as it has in the past when the currency approached similar levels.

## Tech and AI

Ten years of PC gaming history just spilled onto the internet in one massive dump. A 12 terabyte leak, containing builds of nearly every game uploaded to Steam between 2003 and 2013, is circulating online. Modders and archivists are still digging through it, but they've already found cut content from Portal 2 and early assets from the long-canceled Half-Life 2: Episode 3. Valve hasn't commented, but for preservationists, this is essentially a decade of lost development history dropped in their laps at once.

In the courts, Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell are suing Anthropic, the AI company behind the Claude chatbot. The suit, filed in federal court in Northern California, claims Anthropic used tens of thousands of copyrighted songs to train its models without permission. The music publishers are seeking up to 150 thousand dollars per work, with an extra 25 thousand dollars for each instance where copyright information was allegedly stripped out. It's the latest sign that the music industry is following film studios and publishers in going after AI companies directly in court, rather than waiting on licensing deals.

The Debian project, the group behind one of the most widely used Linux distributions, has voted to formally allow contributors to write code with AI assistance. Disclosure of AI use will be optional, but the project says code quality standards remain non-negotiable. It's a notable shift for a community that has historically been cautious about tooling that touches its codebase.

Meta is testing robots inside its own data centers, putting them to work on tasks normally handled by human technicians. The company sees it as a way to reduce reliance on manual labor as it races to build out AI infrastructure. That effort lines up with a broader trend out of China, where manufacturers are pushing humanoid and industrial robots forward quickly, intensifying competition with the US on both AI software and the physical machines that AI increasingly runs.

Off the ground and headed to deep space, the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope launched successfully after years of funding uncertainty and a name change along the way. It's now on a three-month, one-million-mile journey to a gravitationally stable point beyond the Moon called the second Sun-Earth Lagrange point. From there, it will survey the universe looking for clues about dark matter and dark energy. President Trump dialed into NASA's news conference around the launch, a moment Ars Technica notes matters because NASA's science division has only one other major mission left in the pipeline and could use the political attention.

In Texas, Governor Greg Abbott has frozen state funding for new Flock Safety cameras, the AI-powered license plate readers used by police departments nationwide. The move came just before a Texas Tribune investigation revealed the state had already spent over 30 million dollars on the systems, much of it funded by a one-dollar fee tacked onto insurance policies. It's part of a growing backlash against Flock's surveillance reach, even from a governor who has generally supported expanded policing tools.

Amazon Web Services has quietly built cost-cutting networking technology for its data centers, but The Register argues the company undersells just how good it is compared to networks run by rival cloud providers. Meanwhile, a separate Ars Technica investigation asked 100 companies to hand over or delete personal data collected on ordinary users. The results were mixed. Some companies engaged properly with the requests. Others led testers into confusion or dead ends instead of deleting anything at all.

Privacy is also on British minds. A new poll found that two-thirds of people in the UK don't trust their government, or any future one, with access to their encrypted messages. It's a direct response to ongoing pressure from UK officials to weaken encryption in messaging apps for law enforcement access.

On the enterprise side, The Register is tracking what it calls the balkanization of virtualization, the software layer that lets one physical computer run many separate systems at once. VMware, long the dominant player, is losing its grip as rivals split up the market. The real fight now isn't over virtualization itself, but over who captures the growing pile of AI and Kubernetes workloads moving into these systems.

And a few lighter items from the tech world. A UK energy company called SSE spent three years trying to collect a debt from an address that turned out not to exist, only to be shut down by a judge in Oxford, with some help from AI-assisted argument in court.
