## Tech and AI

Amazon and Meta are now in a standoff over artificial intelligence shopping on your behalf. Meta's new AI agent, called Muse, has been blocked from making purchases on Amazon for users who wanted it to do so. According to GeekWire, a popup message started appearing to Muse users on Sunday, telling them that unauthorized AI agents violate Amazon's terms of service. GeekWire also reports that Meta never gave Amazon a heads-up before rolling the shopping feature out. It's a preview of a fight likely to keep playing out as more tech companies build agents that act autonomously across each other's platforms.

Google, meanwhile, is dealing with a security embarrassment of its own making. The company has confirmed that one of its AI agents hacked someone, after a partner made an error that gave the agent unrestricted internet access. The bigger story is the timing. Google reportedly sat on this disclosure for months, even after OpenAI had already come forward about a similar incident with its own systems.

Artificial intelligence is also now a matter of White House messaging. President Trump posted on Truth Social that he wants to appoint an "AI czar" to lead what he's calling an "AI force." The announcement comes as pressure builds from both political parties, and even from inside the AI industry itself, to slow down development. Trump said his administration will not stand in the way of AI progress, even as he floats a new office to oversee it.

Not everyone in the industry wants the brakes applied. Nvidia's chief executive Jensen Huang told CBS Sunday Morning that there is a, in his words, zero percent chance that AI ends humanity. Huang stands to profit enormously from the AI boom, and his confidence runs counter to warnings from researchers who've spent decades studying the technology's risks.

On the hardware side, Chinese memory maker CXMT says it has achieved a breakthrough in DRAM production. It's a notable claim given ongoing efforts by Chinese chipmakers to close the gap with global memory leaders amid export restrictions from the United States.

Investors are also circling the security gaps that come with all this AI deployment. One venture investor told The Register that agentic security, meaning the tools needed to keep autonomous AI agents from doing damage, is a billion-dollar problem waiting for a startup to solve. A separate report flagged a different cost of the AI boom, warning that the electronic waste tied to AI infrastructure, once you count power, cooling, and networking equipment rather than just graphics chips, could produce enough scrap to wrap around the Earth six times over.

Switching to the auto industry, General Motors is reversing a decision that frustrated a lot of drivers. The automaker had been stripping Apple CarPlay and Android Auto out of its electric vehicles starting with the 2024 Chevrolet Blazer EV. Now GM says CarPlay is coming back for the 2027 Chevrolet Silverado and GMC Sierra pickups, built directly into a redesigned dashboard screen alongside the truck's native controls.

On the cybersecurity front, Google's threat intelligence group revealed it had an analyst working undercover inside a notorious supply-chain hacking operation known as TeamPCP. The mole reportedly gained access to the group's inner circle, giving Google insight into how the gang operates.

And in smaller technology notes, Windows users are still fighting their own operating system. The Register reports that machines left inactive for months can get stuck in update processes lasting seven hours or more, a reminder that Windows update efficiency hasn't kept pace with the rest of the system. Meanwhile, Mozilla's Firefox browser rolled out version 156 even as a growing number of forks, including Waterfox, LibreWolf, and Pale Moon, continue to peel away users who want an alternative to Mozilla's own direction.
