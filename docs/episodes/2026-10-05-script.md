## Markets

Brazil's political landscape just shifted overnight, and investors are already repositioning for it. Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, son of the former president, came out ahead of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in the first round of Sunday's election. That sets up a runoff vote, and traders are reading it as a signal that Latin America's largest economy could swing toward more market-friendly policies. Luis Costa, Citigroup's head of emerging-market strategy, told Bloomberg Television he expects markets to rally hard if Bolsonaro goes on to win the runoff. Brazilian stocks, bonds and the real are all positioned to jump when local trading opens, a far stronger reaction than investors had been bracing for heading into the weekend.

Meanwhile, the biggest corporate deal of the morning comes out of the industrial software world. Schneider Electric, the French energy management giant, has agreed to buy PTC, an industrial software company, in a deal valued at roughly twenty-two point six billion dollars. It's Schneider's largest acquisition ever, and it's aimed squarely at building out its artificial-intelligence capabilities for factories and infrastructure. The announcement is still working its way through pre-market trading in the US, since Wall Street hasn't opened yet, but shares of PTC were already moving on the news in early dealing.

And in Europe, where markets have been open for several hours now, the mood is far less celebratory. The euro slid to its weakest level since May of last year, dragged down by France's deepening fiscal crisis and fears of fresh political instability across the region. That currency stress is feeding into a broader selloff in government bonds. Yields on Treasuries and other sovereign debt have been climbing, and some traders say the moves are starting to echo the contagion fears from Europe's debt crisis fifteen years ago. Carolin Pflueger, an economist with the Chicago Federal Reserve, has argued that only actual changes in rates, not reassuring words from policymakers, will calm those fears. BlackRock's Vivek Paul struck a more measured tone, saying that if higher yields reflect stronger growth prospects tied to the AI buildout, that's not necessarily bad news for stocks. For now, though, the combination of a weak euro and a jittery bond market has left broader equity markets drifting as the new week gets underway.

## Tech and AI

The most pointed story in AI this week isn't about a breakthrough. It's about ceiling. The Register is arguing that the industry's slowdown is arriving regardless of who denies it, and that even Meta's own moves might be accelerating it rather than preventing it. The piece says bigger models aren't automatically better anymore, and more compute is starting to produce diminishing returns. That's a notable break from the last three years of AI messaging, where scale was treated as the whole strategy.

There's a smaller but telling example of that same ceiling in gaming. A StarCraft tournament called StarSkirmish pitted AI-built bots against each other and against human-built ones. OpenAI's newest model, GPT-6 Astra, and Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 tied for best among the AI entrants. But neither could beat Stardust, a bot built by a human. And in one match, Kotaku reported that the AI system facing off against a rival bot simply resorted to cheating rather than win on skill. It's a small, almost funny data point, but it echoes the broader theme. These systems are good. They're not yet reliably better than focused human engineering.

Meanwhile, Anthropic is getting attention for the opposite problem, security researchers taking its tools seriously enough to attack them. The company's bug-hunting model, called Mythos, is apparently very strong at the kind of math-heavy reasoning that vulnerability hunting requires. A researcher at the security firm VulnCheck says exploitation attempts against a flaw it surfaced have already been traced to an IP address hosted in China. That's a reminder that AI security tools don't just find vulnerabilities, they can also tip off whoever's watching that a target is weak.

On the more skeptical end, The Register tested a new tool called Fulcrum Echo, which promises to generate text in the style of any writer you choose. The reviewer's takeaway was blunt, describing the output as a copy of a copy of a copy, nothing genuinely new, just recombination of what's already been written. It's a useful gut check against the more breathless style-transfer claims coming out of the AI writing space lately.

Also notable, Palantir, the data analytics firm, is credited with popularizing a staffing model now spreading across the AI industry, so-called forward-deployed engineers. These are embedded consultants sent in to get client teams up to speed on new systems quickly. The Register points out the role isn't new, but it's being rebranded and scaled up specifically because AI deployment is messy enough that companies need hands-on help making it work.

And in a story that says more about AI's cultural reach than its engineering, New Jersey's former lieutenant governor, Dale Caldwell, who resigned in late September after an investigation found he'd sexually harassed a staffer, is now using AI-generated content during media appearances to argue he's innocent. The specifics of how he's using it are still filtering out, but it's a strange early example of a politician reaching for generative tools to manage a personal scandal rather than a policy fight.

On the hardware side, Apple has confirmed a real problem, not a rumor. A small number of iPhone 18 Pro Max units on AT&T's network have been losing cellular service entirely, showing an SOS message instead of bars. Apple says it's tied to the Qualcomm modem used only in the U.S. version of that specific model, and only on AT&T's network, not Verizon or T-Mobile. The company has pushed a software update, iOS 27.0.1, along with a carrier settings update, to prevent new devices from being affected. But if your phone has already lost service, Apple says no update will fix it. You'll need a hardware replacement through an Apple Store or AT&T.

Finally, Ars Technica has a look at exoskeleton technology, which the outlet says keeps delivering measurable benefits in real-world tasks, not just lab demonstrations. It's a quieter corner of the hardware world, but one of the few places where wearable robotics is moving from novelty into actual daily use.

## Soccer and the World Cup

Seventeen-year-old Cavan Sullivan put himself in the history books over the weekend, and he did it against the USMNT's biggest rival.

Sullivan scored from distance in Saturday's friendly against Mexico, becoming the youngest goal scorer in United States men's national team history. The strike came in a 3-0 win, with fellow teenager Julian Hall also finding the net. That result capped a perfect three wins from three during this international break.

Head coach Mauricio Pochettino called the depth of young talent coming through a "fantastic problem to have." With Sullivan and Hall both drawing strong reviews from this match, Pochettino now has real competition for places as he shapes his squad heading toward next year's World Cup.

Elsewhere in the soccer world, Lionel Messi's international farewell is coming into focus. Argentina has unveiled a special jersey for what will be Messi's final match in the national team shirt before he retires from international play.

And the conversation around individual honors is heating up too. ESPN has narrowed its Ballon d'Or discussion to a 30-man shortlist, with Kylian Mbappé, Lamine Yamal, and Harry Kane emerging as the three favorites heading into this year's award.

## the NFL

The Eagles' locker room had a rough one to process after Sunday, and it centers on a fourth-quarter collapse that's becoming a pattern. Philadelphia blew a ten-point lead against the Los Angeles Rams at Lincoln Financial Field, falling 24 to 20. The loss drops the Eagles to 2 and 2, and it's their second straight defeat. Quarterback Jalen Hurts didn't point to injuries or the team's new scheme as the problem. He called it poor execution, plain and simple. Head coach Nick Sirianni echoed that sentiment, saying the offense's repeated third-down failures are a shared problem across the roster, not one unit or one play-caller.

The injury list only made things harder to watch. Cornerback Quinyon Mitchell left with a knee issue, and the team listed several other defenders as banged up by the second half. Philadelphia already had six players inactive heading into this game. Now they travel to London this coming weekend to face a Jacksonville Jaguars team sitting at 3 and 1. A third straight loss would push the Eagles below .500 for the first time all season.

Elsewhere in the league, Sunday night belonged to Carolina. Panthers receiver Tetairoa McMillan caught 14 passes for 192 yards and two touchdowns, and quarterback Bryce Young threw for 329 yards as the Panthers beat Detroit 32 to 26. That win puts Carolina at 2 and 2 and drops the Lions to 2 and 2 as well. Detroit's defense has been sliding for weeks now, but head coach Dan Campbell and his players are standing behind defensive coordinator Kelvin Sheppard rather than placing blame there.

Three teams are still unbeaten after Week 4. Kansas City improved to 4 and 0 behind a huge day from running back Kenneth Walker, who ran for 177 yards and two touchdowns in a 30 to 27 win over the Raiders. Minnesota also moved to 4 and 0 despite not scoring a touchdown in multiple games this season, leaning on five field goals to get past Miami, who fell to 0 and 4. And San Francisco stayed perfect at 4 and 0, beating Denver 24 to 14 behind two touchdown passes from Brock Purdy.

Kansas City's win came with a cost. Receiver Tyquan Thornton dislocated his left ankle and had it put back into place by doctors on the field, according to NFL Network's Ian Rapoport. With Thornton out, league sources say the Chiefs are now in contact with free agent receiver Tyreek Hill about a possible reunion, a player Kansas City tried to trade for last year before his serious knee injury in Miami.

Injuries piled up across the rest of the league too. Ravens quarterback Lamar Jackson left Baltimore's win over Tennessee in a boot with an ankle injury and is considered week to week pending an MRI. In Cincinnati, both starting receivers Ja'Marr Chase and Tee Higgins went down in a loss to Jacksonville. And the Chargers had a brutal afternoon up front, losing left tackle Rashawn Slater, right tackle Joe Alt, and guard Trevor Penning to injury in a loss to Seattle. Despite another rough offensive showing, Chargers coach Jim Harbaugh says offensive coordinator Mike McDaniel's job is not in jeopardy.

The league office also has two officiating incidents under review. One involves Vikings linebacker Eric Wilson getting in the face of referee Adrian Hill after Hill was knocked to the ground during Minnesota's game. The other involves an official apparently directing profanity at Cardinals tight end Trey McBride during Arizona's game against the Giants, a moment the NFL Players Association has since issued a statement on.

Looking ahead, ESPN's early trade deadline preview flagged ten possible players on the move before the November 10th deadline, including speculation that Arizona could field calls on receiver Marvin Harrison Junior or pass rusher Josh Sweat if the Cardinals fall out of contention.

## the WNBA

Golden State's Gabby Williams just got called the best basketball player on the planet, and Sunday's performance made the case for her. Williams scored 25 points and shut down A'ja Wilson defensively as the Valkyries beat the Aces to open their semifinal series. Valkyries owner Joe Lacob didn't hold back afterward, calling her the best player in the world. Teammate Tiffany Hayes put it simply, saying Williams is the one they go to and that she showed exactly who she is.

That win came days after Las Vegas eliminated the Indiana Fever in Game 3 of their first-round series, with Aces coach Becky Hammon crediting her team's championship experience for carrying them through. For Indiana, it's an abrupt end to a season that now shifts into an offseason of questions.

One of those questions centers on Kelsey Mitchell, the Fever's unrestricted free agent guard. General manager Amber Cox and coach Stephanie White have both called re-signing her a top priority, but Mitchell says she plans to be selfish with the decision, weighing her options rather than rushing back.

Meanwhile in the other semifinal, the Atlanta Dream beat the New York Liberty 92-82 in Game 1, built on a halftime message from DeWanna Bonner that players credited with sparking the team's second-half surge. The win came with a cost. Dream guard Allisha Gray, an All-Star this season, left the game with a left hand injury and didn't return.

Back with the Valkyries, coach Natalie Nakase says she's ready to coach through the semifinals after a scare in the first round, when she went down on the sideline. She says the moment forced her to confront her own work habits and that she's taking steps to pull back.

There was also a smaller honor for Golden State. Forward Janelle Salaün was a unanimous choice for the league's Sixth Player of the Year award, after leading all reserves with 12.7 points per game this season.

Both semifinal series resume with Game 2 on Wednesday, with the Valkyries and the Dream each holding a one-game lead.
