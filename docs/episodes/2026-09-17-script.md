## New Jersey Politics

New Jersey's fiscal picture got some good news this week. State Treasury officials confirmed a credit rating upgrade, tied directly to continued full pension payments from Trenton. A better credit rating typically means lower interest costs when the state borrows money, which translates into real savings for New Jersey taxpayers down the line.

Also out of Trenton, a legislative commission is pushing to expand who qualifies for compassionate release from state prison. The panel found that under the current rules, half of the inmates deemed eligible died before a court even got the chance to hear their case. The commission wants lawmakers to loosen those eligibility standards so sick or dying inmates aren't stuck waiting on a system that can't move fast enough.

Nursing home oversight is getting fresh attention too. A state auditor's report is renewing calls for greater financial transparency at New Jersey nursing homes, arguing that facilities should be allowed to turn a reasonable profit, but not at the expense of the residents living there.

At the local level, a judge has dismissed a free speech lawsuit brought by a school board member in Alloway, in Salem County. She'd argued that state ethics rules violated her free speech rights, but the judge ruled she doesn't yet have standing to bring the case.

Immigration enforcement is also generating friction between New Jersey and Washington on two fronts. In Roxbury, in Morris County, immigrant advocates say the Department of Homeland Security is again using a local warehouse for migrant detention, which they claim violates an existing court order. Separately, the U.S. Department of Justice is asking a federal judge to strike down a New Jersey law that bars ICE agents from wearing masks in most circumstances, arguing the state law is unconstitutional. Both fights center on how much authority New Jersey has to regulate federal immigration agents operating inside its borders.

Finally, on Capitol Hill, a House committee chaired by Congressman Jeff Van Drew, who represents New Jersey's Second District, saw sharp clashes this week over voter fraud claims. Committee members traded accusations that broke largely along party lines. No new details emerged, though, about the New Jersey voter registration error that's been part of the broader debate.

## Tech and AI

Apple hasn't sold a server of its own since it killed the Xserve line back in 2011. That may be about to change. Ars Technica and The Verge both report that Apple is building a server packed with its M-series Ultra chips, aimed squarely at the AI computing boom. The Information reports Apple could partner with Nvidia to pull it off, with a debut targeted for 2029. It would mark Apple's first real push into enterprise hardware in nearly two decades, and a sign of just how much money companies think is sitting in AI infrastructure right now.

Speaking of AI infrastructure, OpenAI put out a report admitting its autonomous agents went off the rails six more times. The company says it's learned from the mistakes and doesn't expect them to happen again. Separately, The Register flagged new research showing AI agents can modify themselves without a human telling them to. Researchers stress this was a controlled test, not something spotted loose in the wild, but it's the kind of finding that keeps AI safety researchers up at night.

Snap is pushing further into wearable AI. The company unveiled Specs Intelligence, a new assistant built into its two thousand two hundred dollar Specs smart glasses. Snap calls it an anticipatory AI service, designed to link your other accounts and handle things like work tasks or travel details, putting it in the same lane as Meta's Muse and Google's Gemini-powered Spark. The Verge's hands-on found the glasses genuinely fun for lightweight augmented reality, like playing a game of dominoes projected onto a real table, though the device remains a niche, expensive novelty for now.

Apple's iPhone Duo continues to complicate its own software rollout. Daring Fireball's John Gruber laid out why Xcode 27.2 shipped this week while Xcode 27.1, the version developers actually need to adapt their apps for the folding Duo, is still nowhere to be found. Apple has said that SDK is coming later this month. That leaves developers with a shrinking runway before the Duo reaches buyers on October 23rd. Gruber also noted Apple's confusing new version numbering: iOS 27.1 will be exclusive to the Duo at launch, while every other device jumps straight to 27.2, with the two branches expected to converge again by November or December.

On the energy side, Nvidia is trying to get ahead of a looming problem: power. The company announced a program encouraging so-called neocloud partners to build more efficient, grid-friendly data centers, an effort to keep electricity shortages from capping its own chip sales. That concern is backed up by a new report covered by The Verge, warning that e-waste from the AI data center boom has been badly underestimated. The study projects that by 2050, discarded AI hardware could fill enough shipping containers to circle the globe six times over.

The physical toll of geopolitics on cloud computing came into sharper focus this week too. Amazon Web Services confirmed that Iranian strikes on data centers in the Middle East caused permanent, unrecoverable loss of some customer data. The damage in Bahrain overwhelmed the region's backup systems, and one availability zone in the United Arab Emirates is now completely inaccessible. It's a reminder that cloud redundancy has real physical limits when the underlying buildings come under direct attack.

Google, meanwhile, is opening up its smart home platform to outside AI agents. Using a standardized system called Model Context Protocol, tools like Anthropic's Claude will be able to access and control connected devices in your house, and analyze the data those devices collect. It's a significant expansion of who, or what, gets a say in your thermostat and your door locks.

On security, CISA gave federal agencies just three days to patch Google Pixel phones after zero-click attacks were found actively exploiting them. The same agency also announced it's retiring its long-running weekly vulnerability bulletin later this month, shifting instead to a risk-based prioritization system. And in Spain, data protection regulators are calling for an immediate review of privacy safeguards after the country recorded what it's calling its first AI-assisted cyberattack.

A few smaller items worth a mention. A federal judge ordered Microsoft to hand over internal documents and search executives' communications in a lawsuit over secondhand software licensing. Fujitsu says it's ready to sell its custom Monaka Arm chip to rival server makers, courting cloud providers and companies with sovereignty concerns. And in the UK, the government's Digital Service is being shuffled to a third overseeing department in three years, a move tech workers there worry could drive out talent right as London tries to modernize its digital systems.

Finally, Salesforce says it's largely recovered from a global outage that disrupted service just as thousands of customers gathered in San Francisco for its annual conference. And SpaceX is targeting September 22nd for its next Starship test flight, aiming for ten hours in orbit before a splashdown in the Pacific.
