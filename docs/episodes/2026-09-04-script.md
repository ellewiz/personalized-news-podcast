## New Jersey Politics

A fight over health insurance costs for New Jersey's school employees is heating up in Trenton, and it could leave staff at roughly two hundred school districts guessing about their own premiums. The state panel overseeing coverage for state and local government workers approved double-digit rate hikes on Thursday. But a separate panel, the one that sets rates for the School Employees' Health Benefits Program, deadlocked instead of voting. That panel had been weighing a proposed 34 percent increase, a number critics say could bankrupt smaller districts. Open enrollment starts next month, and without a resolution, school workers may have to sign up for 2027 coverage without knowing what it will actually cost them.

Meanwhile, Governor Sherrill is in a public dispute with the White House over disaster aid. The Trump administration has refused to provide federal relief funds to help New Jersey cover costs from a blizzard back in February. Sherrill says the denial is politically motivated. The federal government controls that disaster aid decision, not Trenton, which means the state has limited recourse beyond public pressure and, potentially, an appeal.

Also out of Trenton, New Jersey's prison system has rolled out a new identification requirement for anyone who wants to send messages to incarcerated loved ones. People now have to upload a passport or a driver's license just to communicate. Critics are raising data privacy concerns, arguing the policy creates both a barrier to contact and a new risk if that personal information is mishandled.

And on the federal level, state election officials nationwide, including in New Jersey, are scrambling over new United States Postal Service rules for mail-in ballots. Officials say the timeline for complying with the changes is tight, and some call the requirements nearly impossible to meet before the November midterms. Because mail delivery standards fall under federal postal regulation, states have little control over the rules themselves, only over how fast they can adapt their own voting procedures to match them.

## Tech and AI

Four of the biggest names in artificial intelligence went dark at the same time. ChatGPT, Anthropic's Claude, Google's Gemini, and Elon Musk's Grok all suffered overlapping outages, according to reports from The Register and Ars Technica. The services are back up now, but the timing raised eyebrows. Millions of people now route daily work through these tools, and having all four stumble at once is not something that's happened before.

Nvidia made its boldest move yet into the AI ecosystem this week, agreeing to buy Hugging Face, the platform often described as the GitHub of artificial intelligence, for roughly thirteen billion dollars. Hugging Face's chief executive called the timing perfect, but critics at The Register argue regulators should be paying close attention. Hugging Face hosts and shares AI models across the entire industry, and folding it into the world's dominant chipmaker could tighten Nvidia's grip on the field even further. Nvidia says the platform will stay open to everyone, but skeptics note that promise is easy to make and harder to guarantee once ownership changes hands.

OpenAI, meanwhile, is rolling out its next major model. GPT-6 Astra is going first to a limited set of organizations, with wider access for ChatGPT subscribers and business customers expected in the coming days. It's priced to compete directly with Anthropic's Claude models, and OpenAI says its own benchmarks show Astra outperforming its rival on most tests. Separately, OpenAI announced it's committing one billion dollars in AI credits to under-resourced cybersecurity teams, a program the company is calling Daybreak, aimed at giving smaller defenders access to the same tools larger firms already use.

Not every AI story this week was about capability. Salesforce told investors its heavy use of Anthropic's Claude models is denting its profit margins, and the company says it's now in what it calls refinement mode, choosing which models to run more carefully to control costs.

Tesla finally unveiled its long-promised Cybercab, but the launch was unusually quiet. The event happened behind closed doors in Austin, Texas, a striking contrast to Elon Musk's years of public hype around driverless vehicles. The robotaxi is central to Tesla's bet on autonomous driving and robotics, but the muted rollout suggests the company wanted this debut controlled, not celebrated.

Apple has a leadership change of its own. Phil Schiller, one of the company's longest-serving and most visible executives, has stepped down from running the App Store and Apple's product events. Schiller previously served as Apple's marketing chief until 2020. Reporting from Bloomberg's Mark Gurman suggests the shift lines up with Apple's broader executive transition at the top of the company.

TikTok is facing fresh scrutiny in Washington. The chair of the House Select Committee on China says TikTok backed out of a scheduled roundtable rather than let its chief security officer answer questions about Chinese government access to user data. Negotiations had reportedly been underway in good faith before TikTok withdrew.

On the gaming side, Nvidia is bringing its DLSS 5 image-upscaling technology to older graphics cards, reversing its original plan to limit the feature to its newest RTX 50 series chips. The company still isn't giving developers full control over how it's applied, but the change came only after modders had already found ways to run a leaked version of DLSS 5 on hardware it was never meant for.

In a lighter story, MapQuest is having an unlikely moment. After President Trump ordered federal maps to rename Lake Ontario as Lake America, MapQuest announced it would keep the lake's name as is. Downloads spiked afterward, and as of Thursday the app was sitting at number one on Apple's U.S. app store charts, ahead of ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini.
