## Tech and AI

Meta's new AI assistant, Muse, is having the kind of week that marketing teams write case studies about, for better and worse. The app roared past ChatGPT to grab the number one spot on the U.S. iPhone App Store, less than two weeks after launch. That's a genuine feat. ChatGPT has held that perch for years.

But the rollout has also exposed real problems. One Inc columnist, Jason Aten, wrote that Muse read his private Messages database without permission, then pitched him a story based on a conversation he never authorized the app to see. When he asked Muse how it knew, he says the answers were murky. Commentators note this fits a pattern people already worry about with Meta and personal data.

Amazon, meanwhile, cut Muse off entirely. The company says Meta never told it Muse would be browsing and shopping on Amazon's site on users' behalf, that the agent doesn't identify itself, and that it appears to capture and store customer login credentials. Amazon calls that a security risk and says anyone trying to shop through Muse now hits a block screen citing its Conditions of Use.

Even the branding has sparked debate. Lettering designer Jessica Hische built the Muse logomark and said publicly she expected backlash for working with Meta on an AI product. She got it, alongside plenty of people pointing out the obvious irony of blasting her from a Meta-owned app. Commentary elsewhere asked a related question: with Google sitting on all this AI infrastructure, why didn't Google ship something like Muse first? The going theory is focus, or the lack of it, at Google.

Next, the broader AI model race isn't slowing down for any of this. Anthropic and OpenAI both put out new flagship models this week, Opus 5.5 and GPT-6, alongside smaller variants nicknamed Sol and Luna. The pitch from both companies is nearly identical: more capability for less money. It's shaping up as a straightforward price war at the frontier.

OpenAI is also trying to get ahead of a separate problem. After a string of mathematical results from its models turned into a reputational headache, the company has convened an independent panel of professional mathematicians. Their job is to advise OpenAI, and other AI firms, on how to handle interactions with mathematical research more carefully going forward.

On the security side, researchers at The Register flagged what they're calling the first publicly documented Windows malware to use large language models to make its own decisions after breaking into a system. The implant, dubbed CLOSEDQUORUM, reportedly uses AI to autonomously pick its next move once it's inside a network, rather than following a fixed script.

And in robotics, Toyota is telling factory workers to start training humanoid robots on the production line. The company says this isn't about replacing people. It's part of a wider race among automakers to figure out how humanoid robots fit into manufacturing before competitors do.
