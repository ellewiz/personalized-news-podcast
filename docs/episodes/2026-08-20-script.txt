The plan sparked a rally in long-dated Treasuries yesterday, but by the close that rally was already running out of steam, and US stock futures were struggling for direction overnight as rising oil prices brought inflation worries back into focus. Analysts are split on what this all means. Some, like UBS Chief Strategist Bhanu Baweja, argue the attempt to keep long-end yields in check gives, in his words, "another lease of life" to the gold trade — since capping yields makes non-yielding assets like gold more attractive. Others are far more skeptical, describing Bessent's intervention as, at best, a circuit breaker for a global bond slump — a temporary patch rather than a fix, with persistent fiscal worries and sticky inflation still lurking underneath. Aegon Asset Management, for one, says it's sticking with its bet that the gap between short- and long-term borrowing costs will keep widening regardless. And there's a currency angle too: some investors now think the dollar could end up the biggest loser from all this, as the government leans on financial engineering to manage its own borrowing costs.

Meanwhile, oil is adding another layer of complexity. Crude extended its gains into a fifth straight day in yesterday's session, after President Trump announced a fresh package of measures aimed at squeezing Iran's economy. That combination — sticky inflation signals from crude, plus a bond market rally already losing momentum — is exactly why futures were choppy overnight, and it will be the key tension for traders to watch when the US cash session gets underway this morning.
