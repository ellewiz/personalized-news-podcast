## Tech and AI

The artificial intelligence safety debate got a lot louder overnight. A senior safety researcher at Anthropic, the AI lab behind the Claude chatbot, said publicly that there's more than a 10 percent chance AI could kill all of humanity by the end of this decade. That post came just hours after a colleague resigned, warning that Anthropic and its rivals are racing to build systems more capable than humans without any real way to control them. The timing put a stark human face on warnings that are usually more abstract.

Meanwhile, Anthropic is also facing a lawsuit. A group of power users has expanded a class action claim, accusing the company of misleading them about what they'd actually get from its top-tier subscription plans. Anthropic says those heavy users are central to its business model, but the plaintiffs say the marketing oversold what they received.

OpenAI had its own rough patch. The company claimed it had solved the Navier-Stokes problem, a fluid dynamics puzzle that mathematicians have wrestled with for about 90 years. That announcement, first reported by the New York Times and Wired, quickly drew skepticism from mathematicians questioning whether the claim holds up. OpenAI also rolled out a new sketch-to-image tool inside ChatGPT, letting users doodle a rough drawing and have the AI turn it into a polished image.

Meta is making its own play to catch up in the AI race. The company unveiled an AI agent called Muse, pitched as a personal assistant simple enough for anyone to use. It's the latest piece of Meta's broader, multi-billion-dollar effort to rebuild its standing against OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google. That push comes as Meta also faces criticism over ads on its platforms that promoted apps capable of generating fake nude images of real teenagers, ads the company was slow to pull down.

In Europe, the French AI company Mistral closed a three-billion-euro funding round led by Samsung, pushing its valuation above twenty-one billion euros. The money is aimed at building what backers are calling Europe's sovereign answer to the American and Chinese AI giants.

On the geopolitical side, US intelligence agencies and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency say Chinese AI firms, including DeepSeek and Alibaba, are relying heavily on what they call industrial-scale distillation, essentially training their models by learning from American systems rather than building from scratch.

Apple's next hardware event is just hours away, and the leaks arrived right on schedule. Bloomberg's Mark Gurman reports Apple's long-rumored foldable phone will be called the iPhone Duo, with pricing starting at two thousand dollars.
