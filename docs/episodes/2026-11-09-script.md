That data lands alongside a separate push on a related front. ## Markets

The AI trade took another hit over the weekend, and it's reshaping how big investors are positioning heading into the US open. Citigroup strategists downgraded US stocks and the global tech sector, and upgraded European equities instead. Their reasoning: Europe has far less exposure to the turbulence now rattling AI-linked names. That turbulence showed up in hard numbers too. Hedge funds sold global technology stocks last week at one of the fastest paces ever recorded, as worries grow that political pushback could derail the data-center buildout that's powered much of this year's tech rally. Nvidia felt that pressure directly, dropping sharply in trading and weighing on futures, even as broader US stocks pointed to a steadier start after the S&P 500 just logged its worst week since April of 2025. Cheaper oil prices are helping offset some of that Nvidia drag.

That data-center anxiety isn't just a trading story, it's a real-world infrastructure one. Norway said it will suspend new electricity connections for data centers while it works out how to manage the strain they put on the national grid. And in Australia, Nextdc Chief Executive Officer Craig Scroggie said he'll make community engagement a top priority, after local opposition delayed a major data-center project in Melbourne and sent the company's stock sharply lower.

Meanwhile in Europe, where markets are in the middle of their trading session, stocks are higher, led by banks. Italy's UniCredit posted earnings that beat expectations, and a merger proposal emerged for Banco BPM, lifting financial shares across the region. That's consistent with the rotation Citigroup's strategists are pointing to, money moving toward Europe and away from the most AI-exposed corners of the US market. Gold is also climbing, supported by a retreating US dollar and falling oil prices, while oil itself is on track for its biggest weekly drop since April of 2020, with diplomatic efforts in the Middle East continuing despite fresh attacks in the region.

## Tech and AI

The biggest tech story right now isn't about a product at all. It's about Nvidia's CEO, Jensen Huang, and the company's board has now opened a formal investigation into him.

Here's what we know. Nvidia confirmed that Huang had what the company first called a close relationship with a woman named Alice Huang, no relation, an executive at a Singapore-based data center company called Megaspeed. The New York Times reported that U.S. intelligence determined Alice Huang was a Chinese spy, recruited by China's Ministry of State Security, and that she was feeding information back to Beijing. Jensen Huang has since acknowledged to Nvidia's board that the relationship was an affair. In a memo to employees, he apologized directly, saying he was unfaithful to his wife and that his actions hurt her and their children.

Nvidia's board says it has, in its words, no reason to believe that national security or company secrets were compromised, but it says its inquiry will examine all available information. The board also says Huang is cooperating with the review. China's embassy has called the Times reporting unsubstantiated. But commentators, including Daring Fireball's John Gruber, have pointed out that an Nvidia spokesman spent weeks publicly defending the relationship as nothing more than routine customer contact, a claim that now looks hard to defend given what the company itself has confirmed. The bigger risk for Nvidia is whether Megaspeed was used to funnel Nvidia hardware into China in violation of U.S. export law. If that turns out to be true, it could become a much bigger problem for Huang than his personal life.

Away from that story, Apple is reportedly pushing to make Siri more capable. Bloomberg's Mark Gurman reports that Apple's CEO, John Ternus, is pressuring the company's AI lead, Craig Federighi, to speed up Siri's roadmap so it can compete with agent-style tools from OpenAI and Meta. Separately, code found in an iOS beta suggests Apple may be planning to let chatbots like ChatGPT or Claude plug into its smart home hub.

In the AI music world, Suno is facing new legal trouble. Rights holders for Elvis Presley and the publisher behind some of Eminem's biggest songs are both suing the AI music company, claiming it generates songs and lyrics that illegally mimic their work.

On the open-source side, Linux creator Linus Torvalds says he's seeing signs that a flood of AI-generated code patches may have peaked, at least for one major Linux project, where fixes are now arriving faster than new bug reports are coming in.

And Huawei says it has built what it's calling a fully Chinese-made PC, using domestic components across the board, a move that lines up with Beijing's ongoing push to reduce reliance on foreign chip technology.
