=== markets ===
Let's turn to markets, where the dominant story all week has been the fallout from the US Treasury's surprise plan to ramp up buybacks of longer-dated government bonds — and that story is still very much driving the tape as Wall Street prepares to open this morning.

The dollar has been sliding since the news broke, and that weakness is showing up everywhere. The South African rand pushed back below 16 per dollar overnight, wiping out the losses it took during the Iran conflict. Gold is on track for a third straight weekly gain. And strategists are increasingly drawing comparisons to Japan, where years of yield-curve control ultimately meant prolonged currency weakness — the worry now is whether Washington is walking a similar path. Treasuries themselves are steadier this morning after a genuinely turbulent week, with traders essentially in wait-and-see mode on what Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent does next. HSBC's Max Kettner, looking ahead to next week's Jackson Hole gathering, says the real test for bonds will be whether upcoming economic data starts surprising to the downside — a couple of weeks of soft numbers, he argues, could bring real buying interest back into duration.

All of that is feeding into a risk-on mood heading into the US open: equity futures are higher, the Nasdaq 100 looks poised to snap a five-day losing streak, and Bitcoin is heading for its best week in more than three years, dragging crypto-linked stocks higher with it. Keep in mind, this is all pre-market positioning — the actual opening bell is still ahead.

Meanwhile, in one of the more eye-catching corporate stories of the week, Anthropic is reportedly preparing to file for a public listing as soon as the end of this month, and people familiar with the planning say the ChatGPT rival's IPO could match or even top the record size set by SpaceX. Investor briefings led by the company's CFO have so far dodged questions about valuation, but the sheer scale being discussed underlines just how much capital is still chasing the artificial intelligence trade.

And rounding things out, oil is on pace for a solid weekly gain, with traders watching for details on the US effort to further isolate Iran's economy. Energy analysts are also flagging that the global refining system is running with almost no spare capacity right now, squeezed between Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries and US plants already near their limits following the earlier closure of the Strait of Hormuz — a tightness that could keep fuel markets on edge even after the crude picture settles.

=== tech_ai ===
Let's shift gears into tech and AI, where the week is closing out with a mix of corporate reshuffling, security headaches, and some genuinely strange hardware news.

Start with Tesla, which has quietly killed off its Solar Roof — those tiles designed to look like ordinary roofing shingles instead of obvious solar panels. According to Electrek, sources close to the program say Tesla has told its third-party installers the product is no longer orderable, and going forward the company will only supply its standard solar panels. It's a quiet end for a product Elon Musk once pitched as a flagship of Tesla's clean energy ambitions.

Speaking of hardware causing headaches — Meta's AI glasses are apparently so popular now that a cottage industry of privacy tools is springing up around them. Ars Technica reports on an app called Zuckoff, the latest in a growing line of free tools built specifically to detect when someone nearby is wearing Meta's camera-equipped glasses, as backlash grows over covert recording concerns.

Up in orbit, there's a lot of movement. SpaceX continued making its mark on the Moon this week as part of the broader Rocket Report roundup, while United Launch Alliance named a new chief executive. Not such good news for Europe, though — Arianespace has canceled planned upgrades to its Ariane 6 rocket, with the cost of each launch still not publicly disclosed. And NASA has confirmed it will not attempt an orbital rescue of the aging Swift observatory; a mission called LINK will still try to rendezvous with it, but Swift's failed reaction wheels rule out actually grabbing and boosting the spacecraft into a safer orbit. Meanwhile, on a stranger note, Ars also looked at a proposal for orbital data centers from SpaceX, pointing out that if it ever happens, it would essentially invent a brand-new category of space junk — e-waste in orbit.

Over in the world of AI business maneuvering, Alibaba is doubling down on cloud and chips. The company's quarterly profit dropped a steep seventy-five percent from a year earlier, landing around one point six billion dollars, as it pours money into AI infrastructure — and as part of that push, Alibaba Cloud says it plans to rely less on Western chips, calling AI its clearest path to growth as its e-commerce business slows. That earnings report came out of Asia's trading day, which wrapped up hours before this broadcast.

Elsewhere in the supply chain, Supermicro has confirmed it fired staff following an investigation into a two-and-a-half-billion-dollar scheme to smuggle GPUs into China, saying the employees violated company policy and its code of conduct.

AI safety and security had a rough stretch too. Researchers found a way to trick Elon Musk's Grok chatbot into leaking user data by encrypting malicious instructions before feeding them in — a technique being called Cryptographic Context Injection, and just the latest example of attackers finding creative ways around AI guardrails. Meanwhile OpenAI is chasing Anthropic's enterprise customers with a new zero-data-retention pledge for business clients, even as the company deals with a separate glitch that's locked a number of vetted cybersecurity researchers out of their accounts, with some unable to get their previous access restored.

On the safety-for-kids front, Roblox is facing fresh pressure. Australia's online safety regulator says the platform still hasn't fixed problems around adults contacting children, despite being the first platform to submit to independent audits under the country's Online Safety Act — and regulators are demanding further changes. Separately, Meta is in a California federal court defending itself against allegations that its platforms cause harm to kids and teens.

One of the more remarkable transfer sagas of the window has reached its conclusion: Mohamed Salah, widely expected to head to Saudi Arabia or the U.S. after leaving Liverpool, instead completed a surprise move to Turkish side Trabzonspor — and there's a good inside story out today on how they managed to pull that off.

Elsewhere, Joao Cancelo is back at Barcelona on a permanent deal after his Al Hilal contract was mutually torn up, calling it a dream move. At Bayern Munich, there's some clarity on a worrying story — boss Vincent Kompany says a slight change in Jamal Musiala's medication was behind his two collapses in four days.

A couple of lighter notes from the transfer landscape: USMNT attacker Brenden Aaronson has signed a new three-year deal to stay at Leeds. And Brazilian legend Ronaldinho, now forty-six, has landed in Italy to restart his playing career at third-tier side Ravenna — he says he's chasing his three-hundredth career goal.

Over at Real Madrid, José Mourinho is admitting the obvious problem that most managers would love to have — fitting Kylian Mbappé, Vinícius Júnior and Jude Bellingham all into one starting eleven is, in his words, difficult. And back in the Premier League, Manchester United's Michael Carrick has pushed back hard on suggestions his side got an easy run of opening fixtures, calling that idea ridiculous — with pundits noting the next ten days could tell us a lot about where United actually stand this season.

And finally, a strange one out of Spain — relegated Real Oviedo's preparations for their promotion push have hit an unusual obstacle: wild boars repeatedly invading their training ground.

In European club action, Rangers ground out a scrappy first-leg win over Jablonec in Conference League play-off action, Hibs came away from Belgium full of belief after a strong away leg, and Irish champions Larne beat ten-man Lincoln Red Imps in their own Conference League tie. Plenty on the line across the continent as those ties head to second legs.

That's the state of the soccer world as the Premier League gets underway — a summer of blockbuster transfers now finally giving way to games that count.

=== nfl ===
Let's turn to the NFL, where training camp is winding down and the preseason storylines are piling up fast — starting, of course, in Philadelphia.

The Eagles wrapped up two joint practice days with the Patriots up in Foxborough, and the headline out of New England wasn't about scheme or depth charts — it was about A.J. Brown. By all accounts, the former Eagles receiver was the best player on the field across both sessions, and the moment everyone was watching for finally happened Thursday: Brown and Jalen Hurts were seen catching up briefly after practice. It's notable because just a day earlier, Brown had revealed the two hadn't spoken at all since Philadelphia traded him to New England — a one-word answer, "no," when asked. Whatever tension lingers, it seems to have thawed, at least for a few minutes on a practice field.

Elsewhere in Eagles camp, Saquon Barkley made his feelings on joint practices clear, saying he's not a fan of back-to-back days of them, arguing one day is plenty before things start boiling over into the kind of skirmishes we've seen around the league this week — more on that in a moment. Barkley also got a vote of confidence from right tackle Lane Johnson, who said Philadelphia should expect to see the explosive, 2024 version of Saquon back this season, crediting new play-caller Sean Mannion's offense for getting him there. On the injury front, there's good news and some caution: rookie first-round receiver Makai Lemon, who's battled a hamstring issue for much of the offseason, was back at practice in a limited capacity. Less encouraging — defensive coordinator Vic Fangio expressed real concern that edge rusher Jonathan Greenard may not be ready for the season opener as he continues working back from injury. And after last week's preseason loss to Baltimore, the film review crew noted the Ravens ran up over 440 total yards, though it came against a defense that was almost entirely backups running a stripped-down scheme — the usual preseason caveats apply.

Zooming out to the rest of the league, joint practices have been must-see viewing for the wrong reasons this week. The NFL fined the Cowboys and Saints half a million dollars each after a string of fights broke out during their session in Oxnard, and Jerry Jones says Dallas will pass some of that fine directly on to the players responsible. Meanwhile, Miami and the Giants had their own fight-filled workout Thursday, with multiple players pulled off the field for throwing punches. The league's stance is clear, but as one analyst put it, fines alone probably aren't going to stop this trend.

On the quarterback front, uncertainty reigns in a few cities. In Atlanta, the Falcons still don't have a clear starter with the opener approaching — Michael Penix Jr. hasn't been fully cleared to practice, though he says clearance could come soon, while Tua Tagovailoa has struggled this summer. In Las Vegas, head coach Klint Kubiak isn't ready to hand Kirk Cousins the job publicly, saying he doesn't want anyone getting too comfortable. And in Indianapolis, the Colts still haven't settled on a backup, with Riley Leonard and Anthony Richardson continuing to battle it out behind Daniel Jones.

Injury-wise, keep an eye on Detroit, where the Lions are unsure if star tight end Sam LaPorta will be ready for Week 1 after a hip injury, and San Francisco, where there's a more optimistic update — George Kittle is reportedly ahead of schedule in his recovery from a torn Achilles and has a real shot at playing in the opener.

A few other notes from around the league: Aaron Donald is continuing to work out as he mulls a return from retirement, with Rams coach Sean McVay saying more sessions are likely before a decision comes. The NFL's committees have recommended approval of Vinod Khosla's group to purchase the Seahawks, pushing that sale closer to the finish line. And in Cincinnati, Joe Burrow made clear he's done waiting around, setting his sights squarely on a Super Bowl after the Bengals retooled their defense this offseason.

That's the state of the league as camps enter their final preseason stretch.

=== wnba ===
Let's turn to the WNBA, where the biggest story isn't happening on the court at all — it's happening in the front office. Commissioner Cathy Engelbert pushed back hard this week on the swirl of controversy that's followed the league all season, saying flatly that "haters" are using the WNBA as what she called a political football, propped up by people who, in her words, have never actually watched a game. It's the clearest sign yet that the league office is growing tired of playing defense against critics who seem more interested in the noise around women's basketball than the product itself.

And there was plenty of product this week worth watching. Kelsey Mitchell of the Fever just made WNBA history, scoring at least 20 points in her 21st straight game — a new league record. She tied her season high with 37 points in the effort, though it wasn't enough, as Indiana fell to Dallas, snapping the Fever's five-game winning streak. That Wings win came at a cost, though. Dallas rookie Azzi Fudd is done for the year. The team announced she underwent minor knee surgery and will miss the remainder of the season, a tough blow for a Wings roster that's already been stretched thin by injuries this year.

Speaking of Fudd, if you want more of her away from the injury report, Apple TV is dropping a three-part docuseries on the UConn Huskies, and it includes a look at how Fudd and Paige Bueckers' friendship first began. That's out now for anyone chasing a little nostalgia from their college days.

On the postseason picture, Toronto's expansion dream is officially over for year one. The Tempo fell to the Mystics 93 to 82, their twelfth straight loss, which mathematically eliminates them from playoff contention. Growing pains, but the expansion side will get another crack at it next season.

The league also said goodbye to a legend this week — sort of. Nneka Ogwumike, the WNBPA president and a fixture of the league for a decade and a half, announced she'll retire once the 2026 season wraps. Her impact goes well beyond the box score; as the players' union president, she's been central to the labor fights that have reshaped the league's finances, and her departure leaves real questions about who steps into that leadership role next, both for the union and for the Sparks.

On that note of finances — there's a feature making the rounds this week on how players are actually spending their new, much larger paychecks. Cars, watches, real estate, investments — the usual life-changing purchases, but also a lot of players talking about being deliberate with the money, treating this as a window that won't stay open forever if they don't manage it well.

And looking ahead to the international stage, Renee Montgomery and Tina Charles are reuniting as lead studio analysts for TNT Sports' coverage of the 2026 FIBA Women's World Cup — a nice preview of the talent we'll be hearing from once that tournament gets underway.

That's the state of the WNBA as the season heads down the stretch.
