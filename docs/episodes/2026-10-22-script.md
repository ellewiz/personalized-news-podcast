## Markets

Markets are waking up to a fresh bout of nerves this morning, and the clearest sign of it is in French government bonds. French bond stress deepened as a broad risk-off move swept through global markets overnight and into Thursday's European session. Investors pulled money out of French debt and into safer assets, German bunds and US Treasuries, pushing yields in those havens lower. The move echoes the kind of caution investors showed during past European debt crises, and it's happening right now in a London and Frankfurt session that's already well underway, hours ahead of the US market open.

Meanwhile, China's stock market has fallen into a bear market. Chinese shares have now lost two point four trillion dollars in value since a peak in May, based on figures reported Thursday. The slide reflects a prolonged slowdown in Chinese consumer spending and growing investor doubt about whether artificial intelligence companies can actually turn a profit. Adding to the pressure, Alibaba led a slide in Hong Kong tech stocks after Chinese authorities summoned e-commerce operators and demanded tighter product-quality standards. That session in Hong Kong and Shanghai wrapped up hours ago, but the losses there are likely to weigh on sentiment as US trading gets underway.

That same unease is showing up in industrial metals. Copper prices slumped as inventories tracked by the London Metal Exchange surged for a second straight session, and a selloff in AI-related tech stocks dragged down risk appetite more broadly. Traders are reading the copper move as a signal that demand expectations tied to the AI buildout may be cooling, which is part of why the same worries rattling Chinese tech stocks are also bleeding into commodity markets ahead of the US open.

## Tech and AI

Nvidia is trying to make sure the AI boom doesn't stall out on its own hardware. The chipmaker has signed Samsung, the South Korean electronics giant, to build substrates for its GPUs. Nvidia is also putting more cash into other suppliers, all aimed at loosening a supply chain that's been stretched thin by demand for AI chips.

On the software side of that same AI boom, IBM's chief executive made a pointed case for running smaller models in-house. He argued open weight models now deliver ninety percent of the performance of the biggest systems, at about ten percent of the cost. He said that performance fits in just two or three server racks, making on-premise AI hard for customers to pass up.

That AI spending is also changing who works inside client companies. Vendors are leaning on what are called forward deployed engineers, specialists who help customers actually implement AI systems. Some earn more than two hundred thousand dollars a year. The Register's reporting raises a basic question for buyers. These engineers often work inside a client's own systems, but they're paid by the vendor, and companies are being urged to put real oversight in place before giving them that kind of access.

Access controls were also the problem at a major pharmacy chain, where The Register reports IT staff and auditors got into a prolonged fight over a Cisco firewall. The password on it was simply the word "password." The piece describes the standoff as a case of misaligned incentives, where neither side had a reason to actually fix the problem.

Weak credentials came up again with eMudhra, a digital certificate authority. The company is defending itself after its private keys were found to be strikingly weak. eMudhra says it passed its security audits and is calling the issue a minor error.

In the UK, officials still can't say how much the government has spent on digital transformation. Despite years of promises to modernize public services, The Register reports there's no clear figure attached to that investment.

The Philippine government has suspended a top technology official over alleged misconduct. He founded an offshore software development firm before entering government, and investigators are looking into whether he steered government contracts back to that former company.

YouTube is pushing back on reports that it's hiding the dislike button. After users on social media shared screenshots showing the dislike count tucked into a three-dot menu, a YouTube spokesperson told The Verge the company is not testing that change.

And the FCC's chair, Brendan Carr, is warning broadcasters not to air what he calls "fake polls," echoing complaints President Trump has made about polling. Former FCC chairs tell Ars Technica the real goal isn't accuracy. They say it's to pressure broadcasters into censoring themselves rather than risk a fight with the agency.
