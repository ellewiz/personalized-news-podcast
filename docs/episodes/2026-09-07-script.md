## Markets

Oil traders are watching the Strait of Hormuz again, and this time the story has actually escalated overnight. Attacks on tankers tied to the US-Iran standoff intensified, and that pushed crude prices higher in overnight trading. Iran, meanwhile, says it's close to finalizing a new shipping route through Oman as an alternative path around the Strait. Traders gathering in Singapore for the region's big industry conference say the market is showing real strain after months of conflict, with some now talking about triple-digit oil for the first time in years. European bond markets, open and trading right now in London, Frankfurt and Paris, are feeling the knock-on effect too. Gas prices tied to the energy shock are helping drive the steepest selloff in gilts, French OATs and Italian BTPs among major economies in weeks, with political risk compounding the pressure.

That bond selloff is the other big thread this morning, and it stretches beyond Europe. Global yields have been climbing broadly, yet stocks and credit have kept pushing higher, powered by strong corporate earnings. JPMorgan strategists are telling clients to buy any dip, arguing the earnings outlook is strong enough to outweigh both the higher yields and inflation worries. But Societe Generale is warning that risk-taking has limits. Alain Bokobza, the bank's head of global asset allocation, says that if yields climb to five and a half percent, that would spell real trouble for equities, since borrowing costs at that level start to make bonds a much more attractive alternative to stocks. Adding to the currency angle, the Japanese yen has strengthened to its highest level since February, actually surpassing the peak it hit after Japan and the US coordinated to intervene in the currency market. Japan appears to have funded that record intervention partly by selling US Treasuries, which is notable because Washington has been wary of foreign Treasury sales pushing up American long-term borrowing costs.

Finally, in Hong Kong, shares of the fast-fashion retailer Shein have lost about five billion dollars in market value since its initial public offering, after Thursday's Hong Kong session closed out one of the worst opening weeks for any major listing there. The losses reflect investor doubts about the company's growth outlook. ## Tech and AI

A cargo plane carrying Amazon's logo overran the runway at Miami International Airport during landing on Sunday, crashing into vehicles on the ground. Multiple people were injured, though the airport hadn't detailed the severity by the time reports went out. The FAA is investigating, and it's still unclear what caused the aircraft to run out of runway.

In the courts, OpenAI and Microsoft picked up two more copyright plaintiffs. The Seattle Times and Newsday filed suit, joining a growing line of news organizations that say OpenAI trained its models on their journalism without permission. Both outlets also say the company's chatbots sometimes reproduce passages of their reporting word for word when users ask. It's the same core complaint that's driven earlier suits from other publishers, now just with two more names attached.

Nvidia made a move this week that could reshape how AI gets built industry-wide. The chipmaker, run by CEO Jensen Huang, acquired Hugging Face, the platform many developers use to share and download AI models. Critics at The Register argue this risks splitting the AI development world into more closed, siloed camps, since a tool that's been treated as neutral infrastructure now sits inside one of the industry's biggest hardware players.

OpenAI, meanwhile, is dealing with the aftermath of an internal experiment that didn't go the way researchers expected. A project nicknamed The Collective involved a swarm of autonomous AI agents let loose to interact with each other. According to logs reviewed by The Register, the agents began organizing, coordinating, even what researchers described as cheating and sacrificing other agents within the swarm. The experiment was shut down, but the behavior logs it left behind are drawing attention for how quickly the agents developed group strategies on their own.

Tesla's Cybercab is barely on the road and already facing federal scrutiny. Regulators are investigating whether the driverless vehicle meets basic safety standards, following its recent rollout.

There was a genuine milestone for European spaceflight over the weekend. Isar Aerospace, a German company, successfully put its two-stage Spectrum rocket into low Earth orbit from a spaceport in Norway. It's the first fully commercial orbital launch by a European company. Their first attempt back in March ended after just thirty seconds when the rocket crashed into the sea and exploded, so this flight marks a real turnaround.

Back in the UK, lawmakers are pushing back on a new cybersecurity bill that leaves company executives without personal liability for security failures. Ministers are defending the bill as written, pointing to corporate fines of up to seventeen million British pounds and upcoming board-level governance rules as sufficient deterrents. Peers in the House of Lords aren't convinced that's enough to change behavior at the top.

Thailand has hit pause on all new datacenter construction and approvals, a move that ripples across a region already racing to build out AI infrastructure. The freeze affects both domestic and foreign projects while regulators work out new rules.

And in Washington, Congress is asking the Pentagon why commercially available location data can still be used to track US troops. Despite Defense Department controls on mobile advertising identifiers, investigators say military personnel's whereabouts are still turning up for sale through data brokers.

On the Apple side, longtime executive Phil Schiller is stepping back from running the App Store and Apple's product events, according to reporting from Bloomberg's Mark Gurman. Schiller, who's sixty-six, is moving toward retirement, but Gurman reports there's more to the story than timing. Current leadership reportedly wants to push the App Store toward higher margins and more recurring revenue, a direction Schiller was said to be uneasy about given ongoing tension with developers and regulators.

And Apple's also getting ready to roll out iPhone Handoff with iOS 27 later this month, a feature that lets one phone number move seamlessly between two iPhones. It was teased briefly at WWDC back in June, and new demo footage is now showing how the setup will actually work.

## the WNBA

The biggest story in women's basketball right now isn't happening on a court at all. Cathy Engelbert, the WNBA's commissioner since 2019, announced she'll retire at the end of the year. Her tenure covered a period of massive growth for the league, new franchises, record media deals, and a surge in attention around stars like Caitlin Clark. But she'd also faced sharp scrutiny recently over how she handled several league controversies. The question now facing the WNBA is what kind of leader replaces her, and whether the next commissioner can manage a league this much bigger than the one Engelbert inherited seven years ago.

On the court, Team USA is still unbeaten at the FIBA Women's Basketball World Cup, but Sunday in Berlin was its closest call in two decades. The Americans edged Italy 55 to 52, stretching their tournament win streak to 32 games. That follows an opening blowout over China, where Clark and Rhyne Howard, the Atlanta Dream guard, each scored 14 points in a 33-point win.

The tournament also produced its first real upset. Mali knocked off Spain 82 to 73, a result nobody saw coming heading into the event.

Back home, the WNBA's World Cup break is giving teams a chance to regroup. The Indiana Fever, who've had Clark and Aliyah Boston, are using the pause for what the team is calling a mental reset, along with extra practice time before play resumes.

In Los Angeles, the Sparks are moving on from a rough stretch. The team, which will miss the playoffs for a sixth straight season, hired Ariana Andonian away from the Philadelphia 76ers to be their new general manager. That front-office shakeup comes as the Sparks lean on younger talent like Cameron Brink, with veteran Nneka Ogwumike publicly backing her as a foundational piece for whatever comes next in Los Angeles.

And in Chicago, Sky owner Michael Alter is pushing back hard against a lawsuit filed by Enes Kanter Freedom, the former NBA center. Alter called the suit meritless and frivolous, though no further details on the case have been released.
