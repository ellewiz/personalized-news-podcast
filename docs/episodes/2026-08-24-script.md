## Tech and AI

Let's start with something out of science fiction that actually happened over the weekend. At the World Humanoid Robot Games in Beijing, robots ran faster than Usain Bolt's world record. In a preliminary heat on Saturday, a robot called Tiangong Ultra, built by the Beijing Humanoid Robot Innovation Center, covered 100 meters in 9.39 seconds. That's faster than Bolt's famous 9.58 second sprint from 2009. Right behind it came another robot, Lightning, developed by Honor, clocking in at 9.47 seconds. Bolt's record still stands for humans, but machines are now officially quicker over that distance.

Staying in China, the country has pulled the plug, at least for now, on its Chang'e-7 moon mission. The spacecraft was due to search for water ice near the lunar south pole. Chinese officials postponed the launch just a day before liftoff, saying it didn't meet unspecified launch requirements. Ars Technica reports the delay could push the mission all the way to 2027, with officials citing a need for what they called absolute success. It's a reminder that even well-funded space programs sometimes choose to wait rather than risk a public failure.

Next, to New Zealand, where Prime Minister Christopher Luxon has proposed banning children under 16 from social media entirely. The bill would make New Zealand one of the strictest countries in the world on youth access to platforms like Instagram and TikTok. It follows similar moves already underway in Australia, and adds to a growing global debate over how young is too young to be online.

In software news, Canonical, the company behind the Ubuntu operating system, is funding research at the University of Bristol to see whether artificial intelligence can safely translate old code written in C into Rust, a newer and more memory-safe programming language. The project will test whether mature, decades-old C code can survive that kind of automated conversion without breaking. It's a small-sounding experiment with big implications, since huge amounts of critical infrastructure software are still written in C.

Also in the developer world, the coding tool Cursor has found a workaround for one of Git's oldest headaches: scalability. According to The Register, Cursor keeps the authoritative version of code stored in Amazon's S3 cloud storage, while local high-speed NVMe drives handle the parts of the work that need to happen instantly. That split lets the system stay fast without losing track of the single true version of a project.

Meanwhile, a long-running legal battle over the origins of Linux, the open-source operating system, is nearly finished for good. An appeals court has upheld a ruling that the case, brought for years by a company called Xinuos claiming rights tied to Unix and Linux code, is dead. Xinuos is reportedly attempting a long-shot bid for a rehearing, but after a design that's dragged on for years, the writing appears to be on the wall.

On the AI commentary front, there's continued fallout over Anthropic's decision to watermark all text generated by its Claude chatbot, subtly adjusting word choices so the output can be identified as AI-generated. Writing on Daring Fireball, John Gruber argues the move likely has less to do with a European Union regulation, as Anthropic has suggested, and more to do with self-interest. The theory: companies like Anthropic and Google want to tag their own AI's output so they can filter it out of future training data, avoiding a degradation problem researchers call model collapse. It's a small technical decision with a big underlying motive, quietly shaping how AI companies protect their own models from being trained on their own machine-made text.

Finally, in gaming, Grand Theft Auto VI remains the industry's most-watched work in progress. Rockstar Games has now pushed the release date twice, first to this past May, and now to November 19th of this year. That's a full year later than originally planned, and it's kept fans and analysts dissecting every trailer and rumor in the meantime.
