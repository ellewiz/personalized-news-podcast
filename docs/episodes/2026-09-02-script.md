## New Jersey Politics

Governor Sherrill put her pen to a new energy bill on Tuesday, and it's aimed squarely at solar power on balconies. The legislation bars towns, landlords, and homeowners associations from blocking residents who want to install portable solar units. It's a small piece of hardware, but the bill removes a real legal obstacle for renters and condo owners who couldn't get permission before.

In Trenton, a Republican state senator is pushing for two things at once. He wants New Jersey to regulate license plate reader cameras, arguing the technology helps solve crimes but can also erode privacy when it's used too broadly. He's also calling for the state to begin requiring voter identification. Both ideas would need to move through the full Legislature before they become law, and right now they're just proposals from one lawmaker.

Also out of Trenton, the state's transportation trust fund is getting a boost. A debt-refinancing move will add 120 million dollars beyond what was originally planned. That fund covers road, bridge, and rail projects across New Jersey, and it's paid for by gasoline tax revenue. More cash flowing in means more room for infrastructure work without raising those taxes further.

An audit is raising questions about Atlantic City Electric, the utility that serves a large part of South Jersey. The findings show the company overbilled customers and made errors interpreting tax law over a five-year stretch, all while rates were climbing. Refunds could now be on the table for affected customers, depending on how regulators respond to the audit's findings.

And a federal appeals court has weighed in on telehealth rules that directly affect New Jersey patients. The panel ruled Monday that out-of-state doctors must hold a New Jersey medical license before they can treat residents here remotely. That closes a workaround some out-of-state physicians had been using, and it means anyone doing virtual visits with a New Jersey patient now has to be licensed in the state itself.

## Markets

Gold fell for a fourth straight session, as investors bet those inflation pressures will push central banks toward further rate hikes rather than cuts. Stocks fell on Tuesday as the conflict kept oil elevated, and futures suggest that pressure hasn't lifted heading into Wednesday's session on Wall Street.

The euro slid to a two-week low against the dollar, and options traders are rushing to buy protection against further declines at a pace not seen since 2017. Analysts at Tradition in Dubai argue the bond selloff itself reflects shifting rate expectations rather than any dysfunction in the Treasury market, but strategists at JPMorgan Private Bank are warning that yields near five to five and a quarter percent could still pose a real risk to stock valuations if they climb further.

Finally, the sale of Portugal's flag carrier TAP is moving into its final stage. Portugal's state holding company, Parpública, has submitted its assessment of binding offers for a minority stake in the airline, with Deutsche Lufthansa and Air France-KLM both still in the running. No final price or closing date has been set yet, but the field has now narrowed to those two bidders as Lisbon works toward a decision.

## Tech and AI

Apple’s campus said goodbye to Tim Cook this week, and he left with a memo that leaned hard on gratitude and light on personal reflection. In it, Cook wrote about Apple’s culture and called the company’s achievements a shared creation, thanking employees for what he described as fifteen years of building something bigger than any one person. Longtime Apple watchers note that’s been the pattern with Cook throughout his tenure. He’s kept his personal life and inner thoughts almost entirely out of view, preferring, in his own words from years ago, that his name never appear in the paper.

Apple is also pressing its legal case against OpenAI over a former engineer named Chang Liu. In a new court filing, Apple says forensic analysis of Liu’s laptop shows he downloaded a confidential Apple circuit schematic and used it in his work at OpenAI. Apple also alleges Liu sent instructions to an OpenAI colleague about destroying evidence once he learned he was under investigation. OpenAI has not yet responded to the specific claims in the filing.

The Federal Trade Commission has accused Amazon of a much bigger scheme. The agency alleges Amazon made twenty billion dollars by manipulating its own ad auctions, replacing genuine winning bids with higher prices the company set itself. The FTC says this happened across billions of individual ad auctions on Amazon's platform.

Meanwhile, Google is reportedly courting Hollywood studios directly, hoping to license copyrighted film and television content to train its AI models. The pitch involves large payouts to studios in exchange for training rights. Industry watchers note the leverage may not run entirely in Google's favor, since studios have shown little urgency to make these deals happen.

Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude, made several announcements at once. It launched new models called Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1, which the company says are up to forty five percent cheaper for complex automated tasks than their predecessor. Anthropic also promised zero data retention for customers, though it's telling clients they'll need to verify that policy is actually being followed. Separately, the company pledged to tighten control over its models and asked business partners to help fund that effort.

Hardware makers had a busy showing at the IFA trade show in Berlin. Acer unveiled a laptop called the Swift Blade 14, weighing under two pounds and positioned as a lighter rival to Apple's MacBook Air. Acer also showed off a concept gaming handheld with an attachable keyboard, aimed at competing with both gaming laptops and Steam Deck style devices. Dell introduced a budget laptop called the Dell 14S, aimed at students and starting with eight gigabytes of memory. And iRobot, now under new ownership after being purchased out of bankruptcy in January, showed a new flagship Roomba that seals itself against carpet for better suction.

Microsoft is preparing to expand a Windows 11 security feature called memory integrity to more devices starting next month. The feature blocks malicious code at the kernel level, but Microsoft has acknowledged it can also reduce gaming performance on some machines.

In the UK, government ministers introduced a new cybersecurity bill that focuses regulation on companies using AI systems rather than the vendors building them. The bill drops earlier proposals for emergency shutdown powers over AI, relying instead on voluntary safeguards from industry.

And Ars Technica reports that a newborn and a child have reportedly died of measles in the United States, though the CDC is not including those deaths in its official count. Historically, it's state health departments, not the federal agency, that determine official case and death tallies.

In space and science news, engineers reviewing NASA's Orion heat shield now say it performed well despite earlier concerns raised after its last flight. NASA is also weighing a Mars program built primarily around helicopter missions, with an early cost estimate of two point one billion dollars, since new landers and rovers aren't currently funded. Then he pulled the plug on the deal himself, after the medical, according to reporting from ESPN. The move collapsed just before the transfer window closed.

Premier League clubs pushed total summer spending past three point one billion pounds, breaking the record set just a year earlier. Manchester City accounted for a big chunk of that, paying a Premier League record-equaling 125 million pounds, or about 169 million dollars, to sign midfielder Enzo Fernandez from Chelsea.

Also on the World Cup front, Josh Kushner, the businessman FIFA had lined up as an investment partner, said he regrets his involvement in FIFA president Gianni Infantino's plan to sell off World Cup assets. That plan ultimately fell apart.
