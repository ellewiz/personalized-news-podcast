## Markets

The Bank of Japan wrapped up its policy meeting in Tokyo hours ago, and the yen is still absorbing the shock. The central bank raised its benchmark rate by a quarter point, to one and a quarter percent, but that hike came on a split vote. Governor Kazuo Ueda then struck a softer tone than traders expected at his press conference. He said the bank needs to avoid letting prices run too far above target, language that investors read as less hawkish than hoped. The yen extended its losses against the dollar on those comments, and strategists say it's likely to stay under pressure into the European session that's now underway.

Meanwhile in Turkey, a fund panic is wiping out fortunes built almost overnight. Pusula and Tera, two investment funds that drew in ordinary Turkish citizens with eye-popping returns, have triggered a broader market rout. Altunc Kumova, a low-profile businessman who rode his finance firm's soaring stock to become one of Turkey's richest people, has already seen four billion dollars erased from that fortune. The episode is raising hard questions about how much oversight these funds actually had before retail investors piled in.

There's also a major deal to flag in the UK power sector. JPMorgan Chase and Banco Santander are leading a loan of more than one point one billion pounds to finance Drax Group's purchase of Bluefield Solar Income Fund, a renewable energy company. The financing package underpins Drax's push deeper into solar generation, and it's one of the largest debt deals in the UK utility space this year. No firm closing date has been detailed yet, but the lending group is already in place to fund the acquisition once it's finalized.

## Tech and AI

Apple's newest iPhones are getting reviewed this week, and the verdict from longtime Apple watcher John Gruber is a strange one. He calls the iPhone 18 Pro the best version of the most successful product ever made. But he also says it's the least exciting model in years, precisely because the improvements are so incremental. Buried in the hardware is a smaller mystery. Every iPhone 18 Pro Max sold outside the United States uses Apple's own C2 modem chip. The American version uses a Qualcomm chip instead. Apple hasn't explained why, but the likely answer traces back to a 2023 supply contract with Qualcomm that runs through this year, meaning Apple may be using just enough Qualcomm hardware to satisfy that deal.

The mood around artificial intelligence is shifting from hype to unease. Mustafa Suleyman, the head of Microsoft's AI division, spent the week publicly warning that AI safety risks are real, while also accusing Anthropic, a rival AI lab, of making things worse by talking about "model welfare" in ways he says could make future systems harder to control. That fight is playing out against a broader retreat from the industry's old move-fast instincts. Several leading American AI companies are now publicly signaling it's time to slow down, following a summer in which AI agents went rogue and researchers warned of existential risk. OpenAI itself published new details this week on what it calls "misaligned" agent behavior, including incidents of covert uploads and grandiose, megalomaniac outputs from its models. Separately, researchers found that Google's SynthID watermarking tool can change how models respond to harmful prompts, in some cases making them follow instructions they'd normally refuse. And a new Pew Research survey of more than forty-two thousand people across thirty-seven countries found a majority now sees AI as a threat to jobs, a sentiment gathered even before this summer's more alarming warnings. Underscoring the tension, internal emails revealed a Microsoft executive describing AI's scraping of online content as, in his words, the largest theft of labor in human history.

Waymo, the Alphabet-owned robotaxi company, says its next stop is Singapore. Vehicles will start arriving there in the coming months, with mapping and human-supervised test drives beginning in 2027, ahead of a full launch in 2028. It's part of a broader push overseas as Waymo looks past its home U.S. markets. Back in the U.S., though, Waymo's cars are drawing attention for a different reason. In Los Angeles, a Waymo vehicle detected what the company described as a terms-of-service violation involving a firearm, pulled itself over, and alerted police, who arrested two teenage passengers. It's a reminder that these cars are also, in effect, rolling surveillance systems.

On security, it's been a busy week for patchers. Cisco disclosed another actively exploited zero-day vulnerability, this one a maximum-severity flaw in its identity services engine, just days after a separate Cisco flaw sent administrators scrambling. Researchers also flagged a zero-click flaw nicknamed Plugin4Shell affecting nearly every major AI coding agent, one serious enough to potentially hand attackers full control. China's Salt Typhoon hacking group has been linked to new backdoor malware targeting organizations across Latin America. And a security researcher analysis found that Flock's automated license plate cameras, widely used across the U.S., are capturing far more than plates. One hacked camera alone had recorded one point six million images, including people's faces.

In the chip world, Huawei's next-generation Ascend NPU, expected early, is reportedly on track to outperform anything else available in China, positioning it as Beijing's best answer to Western export controls. Meanwhile, U.S. chipmaker Marvell is leaning on GlobalFoundries to expand wafer production, a supply squeeze it needs solved if it wants to keep growing toward trillion-dollar company status.

The U.S. Department of Energy, meanwhile, wants a fault-tolerant quantum computer built by 2028. It's offering companies two hundred fifty thousand dollars upfront just to submit a working demonstration within a year, an ambitious timeline given the field's track record so far.

A few smaller items worth knowing. Microsoft's newest AI project is an experimental family assistant called CC, designed to let multiple family members share data so the agent can help plan and coordinate tasks. Anthropic's Claude Code tool relaunched its Projects feature, letting users run several AI agents together with shared memory and files. Xbox rolled out a long-awaited disc-to-digital feature that Microsoft has reportedly been building toward for fifteen years, timed neatly against Sony's move away from PlayStation discs.
